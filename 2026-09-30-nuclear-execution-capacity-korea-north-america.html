Nuclear's Execution Decade: Why Korea Is Becoming North America's Reactor-Building Partner

By Naru · September 30, 2026

Korea has won a path into the U.S. reactor market, Washington is financing a fleet of Westinghouse units, and Ontario has started its largest clean-energy project. Together they show that the binding constraint on nuclear is no longer technology or politics. It is the capacity to build.

For most of the past decade, the nuclear conversation in North America was about whether. In the last two weeks of September 2026, it shifted decisively to how.

On Sept. 22, South Korea's industry ministry told the National Assembly that Seoul and Washington had agreed to revise the intellectual property settlement that had effectively barred the Korean-designed APR1400 reactor from U.S. soil. Up to two APR1400 units are now planned inside a framework of eight new large reactors in the United States, alongside six Westinghouse AP1000s. A day later, Ontario announced more than CAD3 billion in execution contracts for the Pickering refurbishment, which it calls Canada's largest clean-energy infrastructure project. A week earlier, Reuters Events reported on the U.S. Department of Energy's USD17.5 billion supply-chain loan program intended to get ten AP1000 reactors under construction by 2030.

None of these developments is primarily about reactor design. Each is about the industrial machinery required to build at scale: financing for long-lead components, heavy manufacturing, skilled labour and delivery organizations that have recently finished a large nuclear project on time. Korea is unusually well positioned for that competition.

Signals from Korea

The most consequential Korean development is legal rather than technical. Under a settlement signed in January 2025 between Westinghouse Electric, Korea Electric Power Corp. and Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power, Korea had to pay more than 1 trillion won in licensing fees for each reactor export and faced restrictions on where the underlying IP could be used. According to the Seoul Economic Daily, that agreement made APR1400 construction in the United States effectively impossible, and Westinghouse opposed revising it because the Korean reactor competes with its AP1000.

What changed was Washington's assessment of its own needs. The U.S. government concluded it required Korean construction experience to expand domestic reactor building sharply, pressed Westinghouse to reopen the deal, and broke the deadlock. The revised terms permit up to two APR1400 units in the United States, limited to projects under the bilateral strategic investment framework. Talks on Korean companies acquiring a 5 to 10 percent stake in Westinghouse continue in parallel.

The commercial significance is straightforward. KHNP and KEPCO have built APR1400 units in Korea and the UAE and are delivering a project in the Czech Republic, a record of recent, repeat construction that is scarce in the Western world. The IP revision turns it into a product Korea can sell in what is likely to be the largest nuclear market of the next two decades, at roughly USD15 billion per reactor according to Reuters.

Korean analysts are not treating this as a clean win. The Herald Business reported on Sept. 23 that Korean-designed units in the United States would still require paying Westinghouse around USD2 billion per unit in technology fees, fuel and related charges, and that large reactors typically take around 13 years from licensing to completion. The constraint industry sources raised first was people: there is already a shortage of nuclear construction workers, and a U.S. program would compete for labour with the Czech project and two new domestic reactors. Korea's advantage is finite and is being bid for by several markets at once.

The second signal sits further down the supply chain. World Nuclear News reported in late August that Doosan Enerbility signed a contract to supply the core barrel, guard vessel and internal supports for TerraPower's 345 MW Natrium reactor in Kemmerer, Wyoming; HD Hyundai is manufacturing the reactor vessel. Doosan is also part of Westinghouse's AP1000 supplier network and, since 2025, a preferred vendor to Canada's Candu Energy. Korean heavy industry is embedded in the American build-out regardless of which design wins, and Reuters Events analysts named Doosan, BWXT Canada and Hyundai among the small supplier base the AP1000 fleet will rely on.

The third signal is about where Korea is placing its bets. The Herald Business reported on Sept. 28 that Korea's hydrogen fuel-cell vehicle subsidy will fall to 445.7 billion won next year, 38 percent below its 2025 peak, and that the 2026 clean-hydrogen power auction shrank to 500 GWh from 3,000 GWh. The government calls this a realignment toward carbon-reduction impact and economic viability. Read alongside the nuclear news, it looks like a portfolio decision: policy capital is being concentrated on technologies with an export market and a proven cost curve, and large nuclear is the clearest beneficiary.

Korea's nuclear edge is not the reactor drawing. It is the combination of recent construction experience, heavy-forging capacity and a disciplined delivery organization, and all three are now being bid for by the United States, Europe and Korea's own grid at the same time.

North American Perspective

In the United States, the organizing idea is fleet-scale procurement backed by the federal balance sheet. Reuters Events summarized the mechanics on Sept. 16: the DOE's USD17.5 billion loan program targets ten 1.1 GW AP1000 reactors under construction by 2030, in pairs at five sites, with Westinghouse and a utility partner each contributing USD500 million per project. The loans finance reactor vessels and steam generators before financial close so fabrication runs parallel to licensing. The program follows an USD80 billion partnership announced in October 2025 between the U.S. government and Westinghouse's owners, Canada-based Brookfield Asset Management and Cameco, and Westinghouse has since announced plans for an initial public offering. Demand is visible: Wood Mackenzie counts 16 GW of hyperscaler nuclear power purchase agreements to date, and the next step, analysts said, is financing new builds.

The sobering context is the record. The United States has brought just 2.2 GW of entirely new nuclear capacity online in three decades, at Vogtle 3 and 4, and those two AP1000s pushed Westinghouse into bankruptcy protection in 2017 after an 11-year build. The DOE's hypothesis is that committed serial orders let manufacturers capture repeat-build learning, but ten reactors of one model have never been built in the United States over such a short period. Korean participation is one of the few ways to import the experience the hypothesis assumes.

Canada's approach is anchored in existing assets and a national strategy. The federal Nuclear Energy Strategy released on June 22 sets out enabling up to ten new large-scale reactors, with two under construction by 2035 and five more planned by 2040, at least one deployment outside Ontario by 2035, a doubling of the nuclear workforce by 2050 and a doubling of uranium exports by 2035, with a financing policy for new builds due by April 2027.

Ontario is already executing. World Nuclear News reported on Sept. 23 that Pickering units 5 to 8 will be removed from service by the end of this month for refurbishment beginning in January 2027. The province awarded a CAD1.7 billion retube, feeder and boiler replacement contract for Unit 5 to a joint venture of Aecon and Candu Energy, an AtkinsRéalis company, and a CAD1.3 billion turbine-generator contract to Aecon and Siemens Energy Canada. The project will replace 1,520 fuel channels and 48 boilers, return up to 2,200 MW to the grid and is expected to contribute CAD41.6 billion to GDP. Ontario's energy minister framed it explicitly as a tariff-proof, made-in-Ontario supply chain.

New build is under way too. At Darlington, first nuclear concrete for the BWRX-300 was poured on June 26 and OPG is targeting grid connection by the end of 2030, with the Williams Treaties First Nations investing CAD700 million in the project. OPG's Wesleyville site, with up to 10 GW envisaged and large light-water designs under consideration, and Energy Alberta's Peace River project are both in federal impact assessment.

Both countries have concluded that large reactors, not only SMRs, are needed; both are using public financing to de-risk long-lead procurement; and both treat nuclear as a sovereignty asset. The differences matter for market entry. The United States is buying capacity fast, at very large scale, with an accepted role for foreign designs. Canada is building on a CANDU base with roughly 85 percent domestic content, a slower financing timeline and a strong preference for local partnerships.

Connecting the Dots

Why are these developments arriving together? The obvious driver is electricity demand from AI data centres, semiconductor fabs and reshored manufacturing. Korea's first confirmed U.S. investment project is a more than 6 GW gas plant in Encinal, Texas, built to serve AI data centres and chip plants; the nuclear framework sits behind it as the firm, low-carbon supply gas cannot provide indefinitely. But demand alone does not explain the timing. Three other forces have converged.

The first is the exhaustion of the technology debate. After a decade of SMR promotion, Ottawa and Washington have both landed on large reactors as the near-term workhorse. Canada's strategy sets no SMR target; the DOE program is entirely AP1000. Once the design question is settled, the competitive variable becomes who can deliver, and value shifts toward organizations with recent construction records and heavy manufacturers with qualified capacity.

The second is trade geopolitics. Korea's nuclear entry is a component of a USD350 billion investment package negotiated to secure a 15 percent tariff rate, and Ontario's Pickering announcement invoked U.S. tariffs in the same breath as energy security. Nuclear projects have become vehicles for managing trade relationships.

The third is a financeable structure. Hyperscaler PPAs, DOE loans, Canadian loan guarantees, Indigenous equity and bilateral investment vehicles with defined revenue-sharing rules are, collectively, a new capital stack for nuclear. The Korea-U.S. arrangement that keeps a 50-50 revenue split until portfolio-level principal and interest are recovered is an attempt to make first-of-a-kind risk bankable across many projects rather than one. None of these instruments existed at scale when Vogtle was financed.

The result is a market for a scarce industrial input: nuclear execution capacity, meaning heavy forgings, skilled trades, licensed operators and delivery organizations with recent references. Korea has more of it per capita than almost any other country. Canada has a deep but fully employed base concentrated in Ontario. The United States has capital and sites but must rebuild its workforce almost from scratch. That single bottleneck is why the news from Seoul, Ottawa and Washington rhymes.

Industry Implications

For startups, the opportunity is in the picks and shovels of construction, not new reactor designs. The next five years of nuclear spending will go to component fabrication, welding automation, digital twins for construction sequencing, radiation-tolerant robotics, workforce certification and supply-chain traceability. Companies that reduce schedule risk on a refurbishment or a first-of-a-kind build have customers signing billion-dollar contracts this quarter.

For enterprise energy buyers, firm power is becoming a procurement discipline. Sixteen gigawatts of hyperscaler nuclear PPAs have set the template. Large industrial users, including semiconductor, battery and biomanufacturing facilities, should expect to co-invest or sign long-dated offtake to secure reliable clean supply in the 2030s.

For governments, the task is workforce and sequencing. Ontario's refurbishments, Darlington, Wesleyville, Peace River and Saskatchewan will all draw on the same trades within a decade. Jurisdictions that align immigration, apprenticeship and credential recognition with nuclear timelines, and that create pathways for experienced foreign delivery teams, will avoid the labour inflation that derailed Vogtle.

For investors, diligence should look like project finance. The relevant questions are the status of long-lead procurement, the constructor's track record, the treatment of overruns in the revenue-sharing structure, the labour agreement and the interconnection position. Westinghouse's planned IPO, Doosan Enerbility's order book and Canadian firms such as AtkinsRéalis and Aecon are direct ways to hold execution capacity rather than nuclear sentiment. Korea's SMART small reactor, whose Alberta project stalled because no local partner was secured and no first unit had been built, shows that design approval without a construction record has limited market value.

For Korean companies entering North America, the lesson is specific. The APR1400 deal was unlocked because the U.S. needed Korean construction experience; Doosan's Candu Energy agreement came with a commitment to open an Ontario office and evaluate Ontario manufacturing; SMART failed for want of a local partner. Market entry runs through joint ventures with incumbent delivery organizations, local content and a willingness to bring people, not just drawings.

Conclusion

What does this trend mean for North America? It means the region is entering a nuclear construction cycle in which the scarce factor is delivery capacity rather than capital, sites or political will. The economic opportunity is large: eight reactors at roughly USD15 billion each in the United States, a CAD26.8 billion Pickering refurbishment expected to add CAD41.6 billion to Canadian GDP, and a Canadian strategy contemplating up to ten new large reactors. Competitive advantage will go to jurisdictions that convert this spending into durable industry: forging and fabrication capacity, trained trades and delivery organizations with references they can export. The investment outlook is constructive but selective: the structures now in place make large nuclear financeable, but returns will depend on construction performance, so capital should back proven builders and their suppliers before backing announcements.

How could Korean innovation positively impact Canada? The two countries' nuclear capabilities are complementary, and four pathways stand out.

First, refurbishment and life extension. Candu Energy and KHNP are in implementation planning for a future retube of Wolsong units 2 to 4, applying Ontario's methods in Korea. Running that partnership in both directions, with Korean fabricators supplying Ontario components, as Doosan has done with feeder pipes for Pickering, deepens both supply chains.

Second, new-build partnerships. If Ontario or Alberta selects a light-water design for Wesleyville or Peace River, Korean constructors and component makers bring exactly the recent large-reactor experience Canada's strategy says it needs, and Canada's local-partnership requirement gives Canadian firms a structural role in any consortium. Doosan's commitment to explore Ontario manufacturing is the model: Korean capacity landing as Canadian jobs.

Third, fuel and critical inputs. Canada produced roughly 24 percent of global uranium in 2024 and aims to double exports by 2035. Korea's expanding fleet is a natural long-term offtaker, and the two governments could pair uranium supply with joint work on fuel-cycle services and waste management.

Fourth, digital and workforce collaboration. KHNP's April 2026 workshop with OPG, Bruce Power and New Brunswick Power on AI-based monitoring, digital twins and safety robotics points to a joint R&D agenda, and shared training would ease the labour shortage both countries acknowledge.

The actionable insight for Canadian leaders is to treat Korea as an execution partner to be secured, not a competitor to be watched. Korean nuclear capacity is finite and is being contracted now by the United States and Europe. Canadian utilities, EPC firms and investors that lock in joint ventures, supply agreements and workforce pathways in the next 18 months will build the 2030s fleet with partners who have built recently. Those that wait will be bidding against the world's largest nuclear market for the same people.

The winners of nuclear's execution decade will not be the countries with the best reactor on paper. They will be the ones that assembled the builders first.
