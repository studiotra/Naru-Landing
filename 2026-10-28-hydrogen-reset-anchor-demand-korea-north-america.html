The Hydrogen Reset: Korea and North America Trade Megaprojects for Anchor Tenants

By Naru · October 28, 2026

Korea has cut its clean-hydrogen power auction by 92 percent and rewritten the rules to favour domestic technology. Washington has released less than two percent of the money budgeted for its hydrogen hubs' current phase. Alberta has quietly retired the ammonia-by-rail export model it spent five years promoting. None of this is the end of hydrogen. It is the moment the industry stops sizing projects to forecasts and starts sizing them to customers.

Three years ago Korea planned to buy 6,500 gigawatt-hours of clean-hydrogen power a year, the United States funded seven regional hubs, and Canada proposed more than six million tonnes of ammonia a year from Atlantic wind. Over the past month the revised answers have come into view. Korea's 2026 auction opens at 500 gigawatt-hours with half the score reserved for non-price criteria; an independent analysis found US hubs had drawn US$2 million of US$112 million; Alberta's roadmap update conceded its Asian ammonia exports did not proceed. Together they describe a market reorganizing around anchor tenants: a steel mill, a data centre, a fertilizer plant, a return-to-base truck fleet. That is a smaller market than the one promised, and a far more investable one.

Signals from Korea

The clearest signal is the auction. On Oct. 21 Korea's Gas News reported the design of the 2026 Clean Hydrogen Power Supply round, to be formally announced at the end of October: 500 gigawatt-hours a year, enough for one or two co-firing or hydrogen-only units. Awards follow in June 2027 on contracts of three years' preparation plus fifteen years of trading; coal-ammonia co-firing is excluded. The non-price weighting rises from 40 to 50 percent and is scored on technology-development progress, energy security and industrial contribution, a design intended to shift procurement away from overseas hydrogen.

The context is a market that never formed. The Asia Business Daily's "Hydrogen, Broken Ladder" series, published Oct. 12, sets out the arithmetic: the 2024 round awarded a single 750-gigawatt-hour ammonia co-firing project to Korea Southern Power, no award followed in 2025, domestic green hydrogen costs more than 20,000 won a kilogram against a 2030 target of 3,500 won, and electrolysis demonstrations remain around 10 megawatts. Suppliers that invested on government assurances are stuck in a "never-ending valley of death." The 2026 design is Seoul's answer: buy less, buy Korean, and buy from projects that already have steel in the ground.

The second signal is that hydrogen demand is being relocated from power plants to industry. On Oct. 7 President Lee Jae-myung launched the Korea-Green Transformation strategy, a ten-year plan pairing 1,000 trillion won of public fiscal support and climate finance with 220 trillion won of private "signature projects." Hydrogen appears there as an industrial input rather than an imported fuel: a 300,000-tonne hydrogen-reduced ironmaking demonstration by 2030, backed by support for hydrogen production facilities and affordable electricity. The President also accepted an industry proposal to combine tax credits with direct subsidies for companies running early-stage losses. POSCO set out a Pohang-centred cluster linking a HyREX demonstration plant, due by 2028, to the Pohang and Ulsan complexes, stressing that a process running 24 hours a day needs firm electricity and clean hydrogen on schedule, not a merchant market.

The third signal is co-location. Hyundai Motor Group's 8.9-trillion-won Saemangeum programme, examined at a Jeonju symposium on Oct. 12, allocates 5.8 trillion won to an AI data centre scaling to 50,000 GPUs and 1 trillion won to a 200-megawatt PEM electrolysis plant producing about 30,000 tonnes of hydrogen a year, both fed by a solar portfolio Hyundai intends to grow to gigawatt scale by 2035. The stack is more than 90 percent localized, with a national target of one gigawatt. On Sept. 30 Korea East-West Power convened 300 participants in Ulsan around the same idea from the turbine side: 50-percent hydrogen co-firing on an H-class turbine with Doosan Enerbility, a 100-percent hydrogen turbine in development, and electrolysis to absorb renewable surplus.

Korea is not abandoning hydrogen. It is moving the point of demand from a national auction that failed to clear to specific sites where someone else is already paying for the electricity: a steel mill, a data centre, a combined-cycle plant.

North American Perspective

The United States is a year into the same correction. On Oct. 2 Latitude Media reported an analysis by the DOE Alumni Network showing that across the five retained hydrogen hubs the Department of Energy had disbursed US$2 million of the US$112 million planned for their current phase, and that "DOE has not given the Hubs permission to move forward with building projects." On Oct. 13 Inside Climate News traced the consequences in Pennsylvania, where the US$750 million MACH2 hub is in a holding pattern and the US$925 million ARCH2 hub "has gone totally dark." The 45V production credit survived, but construction must now begin by Dec. 31, 2027 rather than 2032. BloombergNEF's Martin Tengler put it plainly: what matters is "whether individual projects are going ahead," and finding customers has "been a challenge, except 45Q."

What Washington funds instead is small and attached to existing systems: on Oct. 14 DOE announced up to US$60 million for biomass intermediates, refinery integration and hydrogen-natural-gas blending pilots. Energy Intelligence's assessment last December, written after ExxonMobil indefinitely delayed its US$7 billion Baytown plant, found that viable projects were "either smaller, local and anchored to coproducts and alternative revenue streams," or "tightly integrated with existing systems and long-term offtakers." Nebraska's Live Oak project shows the pattern: TotalEnergies, TES and three Japanese energy companies are in front-end engineering on a 250-megawatt electrolysis plant that combines hydrogen with biogenic CO₂ from ethanol plants to make 75,000 tonnes a year of pipeline-grade e-methane for Osaka Gas and Toho Gas, with a final investment decision targeted for 2027.

Suppliers are adapting. Plug Power's Oct. 15 update paired record-low quarterly cash burn of about US$28 million with a pipeline of contracted projects, including Hy2gen's 275-megawatt Courant plant in Québec, which turns Hydro-Québec power into ammonium nitrate for mining explosives. Every item on the list has a named offtaker.

Canada's reset is the most candid. Alberta's Hydrogen Roadmap Progress Update, published in July, states that "the hydrogen economy's growth has been slower than expected," that the province produced 2.6 million tonnes of hydrogen in 2025 almost entirely for domestic industry, and that the ammonia-by-rail chain developed with Asian partners for Japanese and Korean utilities met "safety and economic challenges" and did not proceed. The province is moving to a "Phase 2" roadmap focused on segments "where hydrogen is currently most viable," and cites the figure that explains why: only about 3.6 million tonnes a year of clean hydrogen demand worldwide is backed by binding offtake. The flagship that survives is Air Products' C$4.6 billion Edmonton complex, under construction for 2027 on the strength of signed customer contracts.

October's Canadian announcements are small by design. On Oct. 9 Natural Resources Canada committed up to C$2 million to Ontario's Next Hydrogen for higher-efficiency alkaline electrolyser cells, with a first performance milestone due in early 2027. In mid-October Azolla Hydrogen confirmed a C$12.5 million programme, with C$3 million from Emissions Reduction Alberta, to restore 700-bar refuelling in Edmonton after a US operator's station closed in bankruptcy and to add a Calgary station; Edmonton's executive committee voted on Oct. 2 to extend the city's fleet pilot by three years. The Calgary Region Hydrogen Hub's "Minimum Viable Corridor" report, released this month after consulting more than 50 fleets and suppliers, found that fuel-cell trucks cost three to four times a diesel equivalent, that suppliers want five to twenty years of committed demand before building a station, and that "no region has met the bar yet."

Connecting the Dots

Why is the correction landing on both sides of the Pacific in the same quarter? The first reason is that the subsidy architectures built in 2021 and 2022 have reached their decision points together. Korea's auction, the US 45V credit and Canada's hydrogen tax credit were designed to pull forward supply on the assumption that demand would follow. It did not, so each government reset its instrument: Korea by shrinking the auction and scoring domestic capability, Washington by letting a construction deadline prune the pipeline, Alberta by naming the segments it will still support. What remains are projects that could underwrite themselves to a counterparty.

The second reason is that electricity, not the molecule, has become the scarce input. AI data centres, electric arc furnaces and electrolysers compete for the same firm, clean megawatts, and the winners bundle them. Hyundai's Saemangeum plan is the purest expression: compute justifies the solar and the grid connection, and the electrolyser becomes the flexible sink. Alberta's bundle is gas, carbon storage and an industrial heartland already consuming 2.6 million tonnes a year; Live Oak's is cheap renewables, biogenic CO₂ and a Japanese gas grid.

The third reason is industrial policy. Korea's 50-percent non-price weighting, its hybrid tax-credit-and-subsidy proposal and Canada's electrolyser research grant are attempts to keep domestic manufacturing alive through a demand trough so that, when offtake arrives, the equipment is made at home. Hyundai's localized PEM stack and Doosan's hydrogen turbine are the Korean bets; Next Hydrogen's alkaline cells and Azolla's methanol-to-hydrogen units are the Canadian ones. The market is being rebuilt from the supply chain outward, at a scale policy can afford.

Industry Implications

For startups, the fundable unit is now a site, not a strategy. Korea's auction will shortlist on technology progress and industrial contribution before it looks at price; Alberta's roadmap and the Calgary corridor report both point to return-to-base fleets and industrial users as the demand that exists. Companies that can show an anchor customer, a power source and an offtake term sheet will clear a bar export schemes no longer can. The adjacent opening is integration: electrolyser dispatch against data-centre load, turbine blending controls and certification-grade monitoring.

For enterprise buyers, the terms of trade have reversed. With under four million tonnes of global demand under binding contract, a credible offtaker sets the schedule. Steelmakers, fertilizer producers, refiners and hyperscalers that commit volume early can extract price certainty, localization commitments and co-investment unavailable when developers expected to sell into subsidized auctions.

For governments, the question is how to keep manufacturing alive without funding stranded supply. Korea's answer is a small auction with a domestic-content screen and a hybrid subsidy for loss-making early movers. Washington's is to fund blending pilots and let a hard deadline cull the pipeline. Canada's is to fund component research and corridor infrastructure while conceding that exports wait for a viable carrier. The shared risk is that none of these creates enough demand to move the cost curve; the shared fix is aggregating public and industrial demand into regional hubs, as the Calgary report recommends and Korea's clusters already do.

For investors, diligence has shifted from the resource to the counterparty. Value now turns on the credit quality and tenor of offtake, the share of project power that is firm and contracted, the construction-start date against the 45V deadline or Korea's three-year preparation window. Exposure to integrated clusters such as Pohang, Saemangeum or Alberta's heartland is lower risk than exposure to any single hydrogen project.

Conclusion

What does this trend mean for North America? The continent's advantage has shifted from resource abundance to industrial adjacency. The economic opportunity is to supply low-carbon hydrogen into demand that already exists, from Alberta's 2.6 million tonnes of industrial consumption to Gulf Coast refining, Québec's mining-explosives chain and the Midwest's ethanol CO₂, under contract rather than as merchant capacity in search of buyers. The competitive advantage is the stack Korea lacks: low-cost gas, permitted carbon storage, hydroelectric and nuclear power, and a 45Q credit that is the one incentive that has worked. The industrial implication is that low-carbon iron, fertilizer and e-fuels will cluster where that stack is cheapest, favouring Alberta, Louisiana, Texas, Québec and Nebraska. The investment outlook is constructive for contracted industrial hydrogen and its equipment chain, and negative for projects whose revenue depends on an auction or a credit not yet awarded.

For Canada, with the Atlantic export thesis weakened and the Alberta rail-ammonia thesis retired, what remains is the country's largest industrial hydrogen market, a domestic electrolyser developer and corridors being built one station at a time.

How could Korean innovation positively impact Canada? Four pathways are concrete.

First, the anchor-tenant model. Hyundai's pairing of a 50,000-GPU data centre with a 200-megawatt electrolyser is a template for provinces courting AI infrastructure with surplus clean power. Québec, Manitoba and British Columbia have hydroelectric capacity and data-centre demand; a co-location in which the electrolyser provides the flexible load that makes a large grid connection bankable would let Canada sell firm power to compute and hydrogen to industry from the same substation.

Second, equipment manufacturing. Hyundai's one-gigawatt electrolyser target and Doosan's hydrogen turbine need export markets to reach scale, and Canada's investment tax credits apply to equipment rather than development costs. A Korean electrolyser or turbine assembly line in Ontario or Alberta would qualify for Canadian credits while giving Korean suppliers a North American footprint inside the tariff perimeter.

Third, steel. Korea's 300,000-tonne hydrogen-reduced ironmaking demonstration will need high-grade iron ore and, eventually, low-carbon iron. Québec and Labrador produce the ore; Alberta and Québec can produce the hydrogen. A Korean-Canadian green-iron chain, with reduction in Canada and finishing in Pohang, would give POSCO hydrogen it cannot source at home and Canada a value-added export needing no new carrier technology.

Fourth, the Korea-Alberta channel that already exists. Hanwha Energy's April memorandum with Alberta is expected to extend to hydrogen, carriers and carbon management in its next phase, and Hydrogen Canada's million-tonne ammonia project was developed with Korea Southern Power under a 2024 memorandum. With Korea's auction now excluding coal-ammonia co-firing, those relationships should be redirected toward the demand Korea is actually building, hydrogen for steel, chemicals and hydrogen-only turbines, delivered as liquid hydrogen or a carrier that Korean shipyards, the world leaders in cryogenic vessels, are best placed to design.

The actionable insight for Canadian leaders is that the next hydrogen deals will be signed by industrial buyers and infrastructure owners, not national auctions. Provinces that package firm power, carbon storage and an anchor customer into one investable site will attract Korean capital and equipment in this cycle; those waiting for export demand to return are waiting for a market the exporters themselves have stopped planning for.

The hydrogen industry spent the first half of the decade building for the market it was promised. Korea's 500-gigawatt-hour auction, Washington's unspent hub budget and Alberta's retired ammonia plan mark the point where it starts building for the market it has. That market is smaller, industrial and contracted, and it is where the next decade of Korea-Canada cooperation will be won.
