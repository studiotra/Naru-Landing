Capture Is Ready, Storage Is Not: The Pore-Space Problem Linking Korea and North America

By Naru · October 21, 2026

Korea has just mapped its first credible offshore CO₂ reservoirs, while a ministry orders the country's only ready-made storage platform dismantled. Canada's flagship carbon-capture project faces a Nov. 15 deadline for the fiscal terms that will decide whether it is built. On both sides of the Pacific, the binding constraint on carbon management has moved from the smokestack to the subsurface.

For a decade the carbon-capture debate was about whether the chemistry worked and what it cost. That argument is largely settled: amine capture runs at commercial scale on steel, ethanol and gas plants. What is unresolved is everything downstream. Where does the CO₂ go, who permits the hole, who owns the pore space, and what price makes the chain bankable? Between Oct. 7 and Oct. 21, Korea produced three answers to those questions, two of them contradictory. In the same weeks Alberta watched a capture project pause for lack of a carbon price while Washington handed Colorado Class VI permitting authority. Read together, they describe one market with a single bottleneck.

Signals from Korea

The first signal is geological. On Oct. 7 the Korea Institute of Geoscience and Mineral Resources said it had screened eight offshore areas and identified three storage prospects, SA1 to SA3, in the Dongso sub-basin of the Yellow Sea's Gunsan Basin. The work overlaid borehole records on 2,470 line-kilometres of 2D seismic data gathered between 2020 and 2023. SA1 modelled a storage resource of 420 to 460 million tonnes; after conservative pressure constraints, the storable P50 volume is about 92 million tonnes. The vessel Tamhae 3 collected 3D data over SA1 in 2024, and exploratory drilling is planned to move the site from lead to prospect, with a national offshore storage map due by 2035.

The second signal cuts the other way. On Oct. 12 the Korea Economic Daily reported that the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Resources had ordered Korea National Oil Corp. to restore, meaning dismantle, the offshore platform of the depleted Donghae gas field, 58 kilometres off Ulsan, which stopped producing in December 2021 and was the country's designated CCS demonstration site. The National Assembly amended the Submarine Mineral Resources Development Act in July specifically to waive restoration when facilities are reused for CCS or offshore wind. The original 1.2-million-tonne-a-year Donghae plan had failed a preliminary feasibility review; the Climate Ministry had earmarked 40 billion won for a small test bed. Hyundai Engineering and Construction had already completed pre-FEED for the project.

The arithmetic explains the dispute. Korea's 2035 target cuts emissions 61 percent from 2018 levels, and the government assigns 20.3 million tonnes of the 452.8-million-tonne reduction to CCUS. Yet the domestic allowance price sits near 30,000 won a tonne while capture-and-storage costs are estimated at about 200,000 won. No private emitter will fund a storage hub at that spread, which is why industry asked the state to build the first one and why one ministry's decision to scrap a platform that another ministry wanted to reuse matters. The platform's underground reservoir survives; the surface infrastructure that made it cheap to use does not.

The third signal is institutional. On Oct. 21 the Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment published a draft amendment to the enforcement decree of the CCUS Act, open for comment until Nov. 30, allowing it to delegate the planning, management and evaluation of demonstration projects to specialist agencies. The stated purpose is to shift from counting technologies to verifying real reductions, distinguishing tonnes captured from tonnes abated once the energy used in compression, shipping and conversion is netted out. It is a modest rule change with a clear message: Korea intends to measure CCUS like an industry, not a research portfolio.

The fourth signal is that Korea's exportable strength is the midstream. Lacking domestic storage, Korean companies built a hub-and-ship model: the Shepherd CCS consortium of SK, Samsung E&A, Samsung Heavy Industries, Lotte Chemical, GS Energy, KNOC, Hanwha, Shell and Petronas plans to aggregate CO₂ at Ulsan and ship it to Sarawak from 2028. HD Hyundai delivered the world's largest 22,000-cubic-metre liquefied CO₂ carrier in January and won two more vessels for Norway's Northern Lights the same month. Hanwha Ocean signed with Bureau Veritas on Sept. 17 to develop a 40,000-cubic-metre cargo system, and Hyundai E&C joined a state project in July to demonstrate a liquefaction and bunkering hub. Korea cannot yet store carbon at scale, but it can move it.

Korea has spent four years engineering the parts of the carbon chain it could control, capture, liquefaction and ships, while the part it could not control, pore space, remained a research question. This month that question became a political one.

North American Perspective

Canada has the opposite endowment and the same problem. Alberta's Basal Cambrian Sandstone and Nisku formations offer some of the largest onshore storage on the continent; the province awarded hub rights to six developers in 2022, Shell's Atlas hub is under construction and Enbridge signed a sequestration agreement for its Wabamun hub in October 2025. What Canada lacks is a price. In mid-August Heidelberg Materials paused its C$1.36 billion Edmonton cement capture plant, built to send a million tonnes a year to Wabamun, after the capital estimate approached C$2 billion and Alberta carbon credits traded near C$40 to C$45 a tonne against the C$170 the business case assumed for 2030.

The Pathways Alliance shows how governments are responding. A non-binding trilateral memorandum signed July 2 between Ottawa, Alberta and five oil sands producers rescoped the project to 6 million tonnes a year by the mid-2030s, down from 22 million by 2030, with a 650-kilometre pipeline to a Cold Lake storage hub in service by 2032. In exchange, federal investment tax credits of 50 percent on capture and 37.5 percent on transport and storage were extended to 2035, TIER benchmark tightening was eased from 2 to 1 percent a year, and Ottawa agreed to explore operating-cost support. Definitive agreements are due by Nov. 15; Oil Sands Alliance president Kendall Dilling told Reuters the investment decision would follow in late 2027 or early 2028.

The United States is further along, and its constraint is permitting rather than price. The 45Q credit pays US$85 a tonne for saline storage and US$180 for direct air capture, and the 2025 reconciliation law equalized rates for utilization. The result is operating projects: in mid-September ExxonMobil began capturing up to 800,000 tonnes a year from Nucor's direct-reduced-iron plant in Convent, Louisiana, its third third-party CCS operation, and the Railroad Commission of Texas approved its Rose storage site in Jefferson County in a 2-1 vote, three Class VI wells permitted for 5 million tonnes a year and 53 million tonnes over 13 years. The dissenting commissioner said the record had not established long-term safety.

Permitting authority is migrating to the states, unevenly. On Oct. 13 the EPA published a final rule granting Colorado Class VI primacy, effective Nov. 12, the first such approval since Texas in late 2025. Louisiana, which held nearly half the nation's applications, has frozen new submissions since Oct. 15, 2025 under an executive order and is working through a prioritized queue; its regulator said in September that about eight projects were close to approval, while an Oct. 8 hearing on four River Parish Sequestration wells drew organized opposition. Storage, in other words, is being rationed by process.

Demand for durable removals is maturing in parallel. On Oct. 14 Gevo signed a three-year carbon-removal offtake through CEEZER for a Fortune 500 software company, supplied from its North Dakota ethanol plant, where biogenic CO₂ is stored a mile below the facility without a pipeline. On Oct. 9 the Energy Department's National Energy Technology Laboratory funded an 18-month design study for a 100,000-tonne direct-air-capture-and-storage system using Climeworks technology in California, Louisiana and Wyoming. Occidental's 500,000-tonne Stratos plant, once due in 2024, now expects full commissioning around year-end and operations in 2027. The parallel with Korea is exact: capture hardware is ready; the subsurface and its rules set the pace.

Connecting the Dots

Why is the bottleneck surfacing everywhere at once? The first reason is that the industry has reached the stage where storage is the scarce asset. Early projects stored their own CO₂ under their own plants. The current wave, Shepherd, Pathways, Rose, Wabamun, is hub-based: many emitters, one reservoir, shared pipelines or ships. Hubs need pore-space tenure, injection permits, monitoring regimes and liability rules that most jurisdictions wrote only recently: Korea's CCUS Act took effect in 2025, Texas and Colorado won primacy within twelve months, Alberta's sequestration agreements date from 2024 and 2025. The permits are the pipeline.

The second reason is that no carbon price anywhere clears the market, so governments have become the anchor customer. Korea's 30,000-won allowance, Alberta's C$45 credit and even the US$85 federal credit fall short of full-chain costs without support, which is why every jurisdiction is layering instruments: Korea's state-funded hub demonstrations, Canada's tax credits plus operating-cost mechanisms, America's 45Q plus state primacy. The projects that proceed are those where the fiscal stack is contractual, not projected. Heidelberg paused because its price was a forecast; Pathways is waiting for a signature that converts a forecast into terms.

The third reason is that demand has changed shape. AI data centres are turning gas-fired generation with capture into an infrastructure question rather than a climate one, and hyperscalers are the largest buyers of engineered removals: Microsoft's 6.75-million-tonne contract with AtmosClear in Louisiana and the Gevo offtake are the template. Meanwhile the industries that cannot electrify, cement, steel and petrochemicals, dominate both Korea's export economy and Alberta's industrial heartland. Nucor's Convent plant proves that low-carbon iron now has a storage address. Korea's steelmakers, whose allowance costs will rise as free allocation shrinks, are watching.

Industry Implications

For startups, the opening is in measurement and the midstream. Korea's new decree and Louisiana's prioritization rules both reward what can be verified: injection monitoring, plume modelling, life-cycle accounting that separates captured from abated tonnes. Software and sensing companies that make a hub auditable will find buyers in Seoul, Edmonton and Baton Rouge. So will engineers who solve liquefaction, ship-to-shore transfer and buffer storage, where Korean yards have a head start and North American hubs have a gap.

For enterprise, storage access is becoming a contracted input. ExxonMobil's hub model and Northern Lights' long-term charters show that reservoir capacity is now sold like pipeline capacity, in multi-year tranches. Emitters in Korea, Ontario or the Gulf Coast that have not reserved pore space for the early 2030s will be price takers. The practical step is to secure storage optionality now, including cross-border options, before compliance deadlines force the issue.

For governments, the job is to finish the subsurface rules and stop undoing them. Korea's Donghae decision is a case study in how a single ministry can strand a national asset; the Climate Ministry's decree is a case study in how to professionalize the opposite. Canada's test is Nov. 15: whether the operating-cost mechanism in the Pathways agreement is specific enough to bank. The United States' test is whether state primacy accelerates permitting or fragments it.

For investors, diligence has moved underground. The questions that matter are injection-permit status, the tenure and liability regime over the reservoir, the share of the fiscal stack that is contractual, and the counterparty quality of removal offtakes. CDR.fyi data cited by Gevo show only about 3 percent of roughly 50 million contracted removal tonnes have been delivered; delivery risk, not demand, is the variable. Midstream suppliers with order books, such as HD Hyundai's carriers and Samsung E&A's hub engineering, are the lower-beta exposure.

Conclusion

What does this trend mean for North America? It means the continent's geological endowment has become a strategic asset at the moment the world's manufacturing economies discover they lack one. The economic opportunity is storage-as-a-service: Alberta's hubs and Gulf Coast Class VI sites can sell permanent sequestration to domestic steel, cement, fertilizer and gas-power customers today and to foreign emitters shipping liquefied CO₂ tomorrow. The competitive advantage is regulatory maturity: Class VI primacy, Alberta's sequestration tenure and 45Q are a decade ahead of most of Asia. The industrial implication is that low-carbon iron, ammonia and chemicals will cluster where storage is permitted and cheap, favouring Louisiana, Texas and Alberta over regions that must export their carbon. The investment outlook is constructive for permitted storage operators and the midstream, and cautious on capture projects whose economics rest on a projected rather than contracted carbon price.

Canada's position is strong in rock and weak in signal. It has the reservoirs, the hubs and a federal-provincial framework; it lacks a price or operating-support contract developers can finance against, and Heidelberg's pause is the cost of that gap.

How could Korean innovation positively impact Canada? Four pathways are concrete.

First, the midstream. Alberta's hubs have pore space but no liquefaction or marine export capability; Korea has built both for Shepherd and Northern Lights. A Canadian West Coast or Atlantic CO₂ receiving terminal designed with Korean EPCs and shipbuilders would let Canada import carbon from Asian emitters at a storage fee, turning its geology into an export service rather than a domestic cost.

Second, anchor demand for Canadian low-carbon products. Korea excluded coal-ammonia co-firing from its 2026 clean-hydrogen auction, weakening one import route, but its steel, chemical and refining sectors still face a 20.3-million-tonne CCUS obligation they cannot meet at home. Hydrogen Canada's proposed Alberta ammonia plant with Korea Southern Power, and Hanwha Energy's May memorandum with Alberta, point to the model: Korean offtake for Canadian products whose carbon is stored in Canadian reservoirs.

Third, joint verification standards. Korea's decree and Canada's Clean Fuel Regulations both need credible rules for netting energy penalties and crediting cross-border storage. A bilateral monitoring, reporting and verification framework, built with KIGAM and Alberta Innovates, would let Korean emitters count Canadian storage toward Korea's NDC and give Canadian hubs a second compliance market.

Fourth, capital and policy learning. Korea's Donghae episode shows what happens when a storage asset lacks a political owner; Canada's Pathways shows what a negotiated fiscal stack looks like. Korean development banks already co-finance CCS-linked ammonia abroad. Inviting them into Alberta hub equity, alongside Indigenous co-owners at Wabamun, would de-risk Canada's storage build-out and give Korea the reservoir access it has sought since 2022.

The actionable insight for Canadian leaders is that storage capacity is being allocated in multi-year contracts now, and the buyers are not only domestic. Hubs that sign Asian offtake before their 2028 to 2032 in-service dates will reach final investment decisions; hubs that wait for the domestic carbon price to recover will wait for the next cycle.

The carbon-capture industry spent a decade proving it could pull CO₂ out of a flue. The next decade will be decided by who controls the rock it goes into, the ships that carry it and the contracts that pay for it. Korea has the ships. North America has the rock. The contracts are what remain to be written.
