AI Biology's Bottleneck Is Now Physical: Korea and North America Race to Build the Data Factories

By Naru · October 7, 2026

AI can design a protein or shortlist a drug candidate in minutes. It cannot generate the experimental evidence that proves the design works. In the past five weeks, Seoul's record science budget, a KAIST autonomous laboratory built with Thermo Fisher, Washington's cloud-lab network and more than half a billion dollars of North American financings have converged on one conclusion: the scarce asset in AI biology is physical, and the race to build it has begun.

On Sept. 1, South Korea's Ministry of Science and ICT published its largest-ever budget, committing to "SDL infrastructure operated by AI robots for next-generation drugs." On Sept. 24, BigHat Biosciences closed a USD75 million Series C, with LG Technology Ventures participating, for a platform that "generates proprietary, fit-for-purpose biological data at scale." On Sept. 28, Toronto's Biossil became a Canadian unicorn with a USD153 million round led by the OpenAI Startup Fund. On Oct. 1 in Jeju, one of the world's leading biofoundry builders told Korean biotechnologists that "data is the biggest bottleneck." Five days later, Multiply Labs raised USD75 million to put robots in charge of manufacturing the drugs AI discovers. None of these is a model announcement; each concerns the machinery that turns digital hypotheses into measured results, and whoever controls that loop will own the next decade of AI biology.

Signals from Korea

The most consequential Korean signal is fiscal. MSIT's 2027 budget allocates 29.6 trillion won, up 24.5 percent; the AI line rises 84.3 percent to 9.4 trillion won, and the "Seven SEED Projects" for future growth engines grow to 1.34 trillion won from 861.6 billion won. Within that, the advanced biotechnology item funds self-driving laboratory infrastructure for next-generation drugs. DongA Science put total advanced-bio spending at 2.2 trillion won, and the budget names the U.S. Genesis Mission as a target for research collaboration. The significance is that the Korean state is paying for capital expenditure that in the United States is mostly venture-funded, creating facilities foreign partners can plug into.

The second signal shows what that infrastructure looks like. On Aug. 26, KAIST signed a memorandum of understanding with Thermo Fisher Scientific to build the SHARE Hub, a closed-loop autonomous laboratory in which researchers define objectives, AI develops the experimental strategy, robots execute the workflow and AI analyses the results to guide the next iteration. The partners intend to open the hub to companies, universities and hospitals in Korea and abroad as "a global reference site for autonomous discovery." An instrument maker choosing Daejeon as that site says where it expects demand.

The third signal is about models and their limits. On Sept. 9, KAIST professor Kim Woo-youn described K-Fold, a protein-structure model built under MSIT's foundation model programme. His commercial argument: AlphaFold3 restricts commercial use and replicas already exist in the U.S. and China, so a proprietary model has real value. His constraint: experimentally determined protein structures total roughly 200,000 after decades of global effort, and toxicity and clinical data remain locked inside pharmaceutical companies. "Newly obtaining biological data takes a great deal of time and money," he said, "so increasing it indefinitely is virtually impossible."

The fourth signal came from an American. At the Korean Society for Biotechnology's fall meeting on Oct. 1, Huimin Zhao of the University of Illinois said his NSF Global Center for Biofoundry Applications has collaborated for years with the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology on autonomous laboratories and that the two countries' goals are "very similar." Korea's biofoundry build-out had emphasised workflows, he observed; it should now prioritise AI integration and pool data across institutions. "Korea is fortunate in that it has funding. What matters is setting the right priorities."

The fifth signal is on the manufacturing side. Lotte Biologics' 120,000-litre Songdo Plant 1 was designed around an automated control system and a digital twin, targets GMP-ready status in November, and is marketed as a dual-site model pairing a clinical-stage plant in Syracuse, New York, with commercial production in Korea. At Bio Japan this week, Lotte and Samsung Biologics are pitching simulation and digital capabilities rather than tank volume alone. Korea's bioprocess sector is being rebuilt as a data business with a North American front door.

Korea's advantage in AI biology is not a better model. It is a government willing to pay for the robots, universities that can operate them, and manufacturers already designing plants around the data those robots will produce.

North American Perspective

In the United States, the public sector moved first and at scale. On July 22, the National Science Foundation awarded USD380 million to 20 teams to build a nationwide network of AI-enabled automated laboratories, matched by more than USD20 million from the Astera Institute. The NSF framed it as a core contribution to the federal Genesis Mission and as implementation of a recommendation from the bipartisan National Security Commission on Emerging Biotechnology. The companion Cloud LAB Act, now before both chambers, would direct the NSF to "generate high-quality biological data through automated experimentation" for training AI, and the commission names China's collection of biological data as the reason.

Private capital reached the same conclusion from the other direction. Enveda's USD311 million Series E on Sept. 23, led by Catalio Capital Management at a USD2 billion valuation, funds AI-discovered natural-product drugs now in patients; TechCrunch noted that no AI-discovered drug has yet been approved by the FDA. BigHat's Series C, co-led by DFJ Growth and Premji Invest, takes it to USD223 million raised; its lead asset, BHB810, is an antibody-drug conjugate in Phase 1 and one of the first AI-designed biologics in humans. Premji Invest described the thesis precisely: a "data advantage that compounds with every program."

The infrastructure layer is commercialising too. On Sept. 17, Ginkgo Bioworks' Datapoints unit agreed to supply developability data in machine-learning-ready formats to companies on Lilly's TuneLab platform, closing the loop between hypothesis and validated data "in days rather than months." On Oct. 6, Multiply Labs closed a USD75 million Series B led by Patrick Soon-Shiong's NantWorks, with AstraZeneca and Teradyne as new investors, to scale robotic clusters that automate biologics manufacturing inside GMP-compliant enclosures. It sells the clusters for pharmaceutical companies to own, so production data stays in-house. Its line that "physical AI can be as impactful for drug manufacturing as digital AI is for drug discovery" could have been lifted from Korea's budget.

Canada's signals are strong on talent and translation and thinner on infrastructure. Biossil's USD153 million round at a USD1 billion valuation, led by OpenAI's venture fund with Founders Fund participating, backs a Toronto company that acquires drug candidates that failed late-stage trials and uses AI to find the patients and indications where they should have worked. Co-founder Anthony Mouchantaf's framing matters more than the valuation: AI insights "need to be translated into the physical world," which requires "dedicated infrastructure capable of integrating AI and medicine together from discovery through development and into the clinic," and Biossil is "building that infrastructure in the United States and Canada."

On the public side, Ottawa announced CAD67 million through the Life Sciences Fund on Sept. 18, framed as dual-use capacity under the Defence Industrial Strategy. The components are inputs rather than platforms: up to CAD2.8 million for STEMCELL Technologies to make cell-culture media domestically, up to CAD6 million for Northern RNA's GMP capacity for oligonucleotides and self-amplifying RNA, and top-ups for the five pan-Canadian biomanufacturing hubs. Research Money reported on Sept. 30 that those hubs, backed by CAD584 million in federal commitments since 2022, are building GMP laboratories and training programmes, with the University of Toronto hub mandated to pursue AI-driven process innovation.

Both governments treat biological data and biomanufacturing as sovereignty assets. The difference is the instrument. The United States is funding data generation directly. Canada is funding supply-chain resilience and clinical translation, and has no equivalent of the NSF cloud-lab network or Korea's self-driving laboratory line item.

Connecting the Dots

Why is this happening in Seoul, Washington, San Mateo and Toronto at once? Because one part of AI biology has commoditised while another has not. In-silico design is abundant: replicas of AlphaFold3 exist in the U.S. and China, BigHat partners with "leading frontier AI companies" for agentic design and Biossil runs on OpenAI's models. When prediction is cheap, value migrates to what remains scarce, and that is validated experimental data.

The second force is the convergence of biology with physical AI, manufacturing and infrastructure. Korea's budget makes physical AI one of three national megaprojects with 2.6 trillion won for demonstrations and places it in the same document as a 10,000-GPU sovereign AI programme and AI data-centre testbeds; Zhao predicted that AI directly controlling instruments will be the next stage of biofoundries; Multiply Labs calls itself a physical AI company. The robotics and data stack built for factories is the stack an autonomous laboratory needs. Autonomous labs also compete for the same compute and power as the data centres we have tracked in previous issues.

The third force is the translation gap and the capital that follows it. With no AI-discovered drug approved, money is moving toward companies that de-risk the physical steps: Enveda and BigHat into the clinic, Multiply Labs into manufacturing, Biossil into molecules whose expensive clinical data already exists. The tell in each round is the strategic investor list: LG Technology Ventures and Lilly in BigHat, AstraZeneca and Teradyne in Multiply Labs, OpenAI in Biossil, Thermo Fisher beside KAIST. Instrument makers, pharmaceutical companies and frontier AI labs are buying positions in the data-generation layer because each needs the data more than another model. Underneath it all is geopolitics: the NSCEB frames cloud labs as a response to China, Canada funds life sciences under a defence strategy, Korea frames its AI budget as sovereign capability. Biological data is becoming a resource governments count, protect and trade.

Industry Implications

For startups, the question is whether you own a data loop or rent one. Building another protein model is a losing proposition against frontier labs and national programmes. Building a platform that generates proprietary, validated data for a defined problem class, as BigHat has for protein developability, is fundable at premium valuations. Founders who cannot afford robots now have shared facilities: Ginkgo's cloud lab through TuneLab, the NSF test-bed sites and, for those willing to work internationally, KAIST's SHARE Hub.

For enterprise, data access is becoming the currency of partnership. Lilly's TuneLab is the template: access to models trained on decades of proprietary data in exchange for partners generating new data in Lilly's formats. CDMOs face a sharper challenge: Lotte and Samsung are right to sell digital twins alongside capacity, because Multiply Labs' owned-cluster model proposes that pharma keep manufacturing and its data in-house.

For governments, the job is standards, access and security, not just capex. Zhao's point that Korean data must be pooled across institutions to be useful for training is the policy problem in one sentence. Governments that define AI-ready data standards, run autonomous labs as shared user facilities and treat them as critical infrastructure, as the NSCEB has urged, will attract the companies. Canada has the manufacturing and clinical pieces of this agenda and lacks the data-generation piece.

For investors, diligence should look like a factory audit. The questions that matter are cost per validated data point, cycle time from hypothesis to result, the share of the workflow that is genuinely autonomous rather than automated, whether the data is proprietary or shared, and whether a clinical readout is near. BHB810's Phase 1 and Enveda's trials are the first real tests of whether the loop produces medicines. Strategic participation in a round is the strongest external signal that the data is real.

Conclusion

What does this trend mean for North America? It means the region's advantage in AI biology is shifting from writing the models to owning the places where models are tested. The economic opportunity is to become the global venue where biological hypotheses are generated, validated and manufactured, and the United States has moved to secure it with a USD380 million national cloud-lab network, bipartisan legislation and a venture market that financed more than USD500 million of data-loop and translation companies between Sept. 23 and Sept. 28 alone. Its competitive advantages are frontier AI labs, the deepest pharmaceutical partner base and a policy consensus that treats biological data as a security asset. The industrial implication is that biomanufacturing is about to become a robotics and data industry, which favours North American strengths in automation and software and threatens incumbents whose edge was volume. The investment outlook is constructive for platforms with proprietary data and clinical catalysts, and selective on capital-intensive laboratories without customers.

Canada's position is more exposed. Its clinical institutions, talent and cell-culture and RNA manufacturing base are precisely the inputs a data loop needs. But without domestic data-generation infrastructure, Canada risks supplying the patients, scientists and cell media for loops that are owned, financed and trained elsewhere. That gap is where Korea becomes relevant.

How could Korean innovation positively impact Canada? Four pathways stand out.

First, joint R&D. KAIST and Thermo Fisher intend to open the SHARE Hub to foreign hospitals, institutes and companies. Canada's biomanufacturing hubs, particularly the University of Toronto hub with its AI-driven process mandate, could become the first North American nodes, exchanging clinical and process data for access to autonomous experimentation Canada has not funded itself.

Second, manufacturing. Lotte Biologics' dual-site model, pairing a North American clinical-stage plant with Korean commercial capacity, could be replicated with Canadian facilities, and Korea's digital-twin expertise is directly relevant to the GMP laboratories Canada's hubs are building. In the other direction, STEMCELL's media and Northern RNA's oligonucleotide capacity are inputs Korean biofoundries and CDMOs need from allied suppliers.

Third, AI infrastructure, data and policy. Korea's budget names the U.S. Genesis Mission as a collaboration partner; Canada should ensure it appears on that list too. A bilateral agreement covering AI-ready biological data standards and reciprocal access to autonomous laboratories would give Canadian researchers data-generation capacity and give Korea the diverse clinical data its own researchers say they lack.

Fourth, investment. LG Technology Ventures' participation in BigHat shows Korean strategics already fund North American data-loop companies. Canadian founders should treat LG, the Samsung Life Science Fund and Korea's other corporate venture arms as targets, and Canadian funds should look at the K-Fold generation of Korean bio-AI spinouts, which will need North American clinical partners.

The actionable insight for Canadian leaders is that Korea's self-driving laboratory budget will be allocated to partners over the next 18 months, and the KAIST hub will choose its first foreign reference partners in the same window. Canadian hubs, hospitals and investors that sign institutional agreements now will acquire data-generation capacity at a fraction of the cost of building it.

The next decade of AI biology will not be won by the country with the best model. It will be won by the countries that built the laboratories where models learn, and agreed early on whose data they could learn from.
