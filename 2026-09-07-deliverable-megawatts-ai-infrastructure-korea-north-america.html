The Next AI Moat Is the Deliverable Megawatt

By Naru · September 7, 2026

Korea and North America are learning the same lesson: AI leadership now depends as much on power, water, transmission and siting as it does on chips or models.

The first phase of the AI boom was measured in GPUs. The next phase will be measured in deliverable megawatts.

That shift is visible in two very different markets. In South Korea, the government is trying to turn AI data centers into national infrastructure by streamlining permits, steering projects outside the Seoul capital region, reviewing additional nuclear power and small modular reactors, and tying AI capacity to a broader industrial strategy for semiconductors and advanced manufacturing. At the same time, one of the highest-profile private AI infrastructure initiatives associated with OpenAI, Samsung and SK has reportedly stalled over precisely the issues that now define the sector: power, water, site control and financing.

In North America, the same collision is playing out through a more fragmented regulatory system. Canada has launched national responsible data-centre development principles that put ratepayer protection, water stewardship, transparency and local benefits at the centre of project evaluation. The United States is confronting transmission congestion, speculative interconnection queues and state-level rules requiring large loads to behave less like passive customers and more like active grid participants.

Together, these developments point to a larger market movement. AI infrastructure is no longer a real-estate category or a cloud-computing procurement line. It is becoming an energy, manufacturing and industrial-policy problem. That creates risk for developers moving faster than grids can expand. It also creates opportunity for companies that can help turn electricity from a bottleneck into a bankable advantage.

Signals from Korea

South Korea is approaching AI infrastructure with the instincts of an industrial power. The country understands concentrated infrastructure: shipyards, battery plants, semiconductor clusters, telecom networks and globally scaled manufacturing ecosystems. The question is whether that operating model can be adapted to AI data centers, where power and water are strategic inputs rather than utilities in the background.

The strongest policy signal came from the Ministry of Science and ICT's announcement that the Special Act on the Promotion of the Artificial Intelligence Data Center Industry passed the National Assembly plenary session on May 7, 2026. The law introduces batch processing for permits and approvals, a timeout system that can deem approvals granted after a set period, exemptions from power-grid impact assessments for qualifying projects in non-capital regions, and relaxed facility standards for server-oriented buildings. It is scheduled to take effect in February 2027.

For executives and investors, the commercial significance is clear. Korea is not treating AI data centers as ordinary commercial property. It is creating a legal framework to reduce administrative friction, encourage regional deployment and attract large-scale domestic and foreign investment. In an AI infrastructure market, time-to-power has become as important as time-to-market.

But policy acceleration does not eliminate physics. Korea JoongAng Daily reported on Sept. 7 that the Korean leg of OpenAI's Stargate-related data-center plans with Samsung SDS and SK Telecom had made little concrete progress nearly a year after being announced. The report said sites, power supply and funding structures remained unsettled, with an industry source pointing to the difficulty of securing locations that can handle the necessary power and water.

That story separates announcement value from execution value. Korea has world-class memory-chip companies, engineering capacity and strong public ambition around AI. Yet even there, a data-center project can be slowed by energy, water and capital structure. The lesson is that every industrial ecosystem is being forced to redesign the interface between compute and utilities.

The third Korean signal is power planning. The Herald Business reported in July that Korea's climate and energy leadership had opened the door to additional nuclear plants and SMRs as the country looks at a possible power gap of more than 50 GW by 2040. The same discussion included a target to expand renewable generation capacity to 100 GW by 2030 while integrating nuclear power in parallel.

This is the strategic core of Korea's position. AI demand is not isolated from semiconductor demand, electrification or advanced manufacturing. It sits on top of them. Korea is therefore trying to build an integrated answer: faster data-center permitting, regional siting, more renewable generation, a renewed nuclear conversation, water-resource planning and grid reform. The approach is not without social, cost or environmental trade-offs. But it recognizes that AI infrastructure cannot be scaled by cloud companies alone.

For Korea, the AI race is becoming a system-design problem: chips, data centers, nuclear, renewables, transmission, water and regional development all have to move together.

North American Perspective

North America is wrestling with the same problem through a different institutional architecture. Instead of a single national industrial plan, developers face federal studies, provincial frameworks, state utility commissions, municipal approvals, market operators and community resistance. The opportunity is large, but the route to execution is uneven.

Canada's Sept. 3 launch of Responsible Data Centre Development Principles is one of the clearest recent signals. The federal framework says data centres must create lasting local benefits, avoid shifting electricity costs to Canadians, minimize water use and environmental impacts, be transparent about local impacts, and bring strategic value to Canada. The official ISED principles go further by stating that proponents should pay the costs of connecting and serving their projects, including new generation, transmission, substations and grid upgrades directly attributable to their development, proportionate to their scale and impact.

That is a notable shift in tone. Canada wants AI infrastructure, but it is defining the terms of social licence. A data centre cannot simply arrive with a load request and assume the public will absorb the grid cost. It must explain how it benefits the host community, how it uses water, whether it strengthens sovereign compute capacity, and how it avoids raising electricity costs for households and existing businesses. For Korean innovators evaluating Canada, that raises the standard for market entry. It also creates openings for technologies that reduce water intensity, improve energy efficiency, add storage, enable demand flexibility, strengthen grid equipment or pair compute with clean generation.

In the United States, the problem is more acute in some regions because demand requests are enormous and not always reliable. Reuters reported on Sept. 1 that electricity requests from very large power users, mostly data centers, exceed 700 GW across parts of the Midwest, Mid-Atlantic and South, more than 10 times some estimates of current U.S. data-center power use. Texas requests alone reportedly rose from roughly 48 GW in 2023 to more than 474 GW. Reuters framed the issue as a reckoning over possible “ghost demand”: projects seeking grid positions before their financing, customers or permits are fully real.

The investment implication is significant. Utilities and regulators can no longer treat every interconnection request as equally bankable. Developers with signed offtake, credible financing, flexible load strategies and clear local benefits will increasingly be separated from speculative queue holders. Proof of deliverability will matter more than generic AI exposure.

The U.S. Department of Energy's July draft National Transmission Needs Study adds a federal planning lens. The DOE said additional transmission is needed because of load growth from data centers, domestic manufacturing, large industrial loads and the broader economy. The study identifies transmission needs across regions and notes that transmission must support reliability, new generation interconnection, new load interconnection and congestion relief. A Data Center Knowledge analysis of the study put the market point plainly: at hundreds of megawatts, an AI campus becomes a transmission-planning event, not just a customer connection.

Texas is also beginning to define operating rules for the AI load era. Utility Dive reported that the Public Utility Commission of Texas approved a 260-MW AI data center co-located with a wind farm, but required the facility to curtail its full load within 30 minutes during grid emergencies and barred compensation through certain demand-response arrangements tied to the order. That is a preview of a wider regulatory bargain: large loads may get faster or more tailored access to power if they can prove they will not weaken reliability when the system is stressed.

The North American picture is therefore less coordinated than Korea's, but just as consequential. Canada is emphasizing responsible development and ratepayer protection. The United States is forcing clarity around queues, transmission, co-location and reliability. Both markets are moving toward the same commercial filter: if a project cannot show how it will secure power, pay for grid impacts, manage water and operate flexibly, it will face delays, higher costs or political resistance.

Connecting the Dots

The common trend is not simply that AI uses more electricity. The larger movement is that AI is collapsing the boundary between digital strategy and physical infrastructure.

For two decades, software benefited from abstraction. Cloud capacity was purchased on demand, and founders rarely thought about substations, generation capacity or transmission congestion. AI changes that equation. Training and inference workloads are turning compute into a heavy industrial load, closer to semiconductor fabrication than traditional software hosting.

That explains why these developments are occurring simultaneously. AI model competition is increasing compute demand. Advanced manufacturing policies in Korea, Canada and the United States are adding electricity load through semiconductor, battery, critical-mineral and clean-tech supply chains. Electrification is raising demand across transportation, buildings and industry. Synthetic biology and AI drug discovery are also becoming more compute-intensive as companies run larger foundation models for proteins, cells, materials and automated laboratories. The physical inputs behind digital innovation are expanding at the same time.

Manufacturing matters because the buildout depends on transformers, switchgear, high-voltage equipment, cooling systems, power electronics, batteries, grid software and construction labour. Korea is relevant not only as a data-center host, but as a supplier and partner for systems North America needs. Korean strengths in electrical equipment, batteries, semiconductors, EPC execution and automation map directly onto North American bottlenecks.

Investment is following the same logic. The most attractive AI-infrastructure opportunities may be enabling layers: grid-interconnection analytics, substation components, liquid cooling, behind-the-meter clean power, storage, modular nuclear supply chains, demand-response orchestration and water reuse. The picks-and-shovels market is moving from chips alone to the physical systems that allow chips to run.

There is also a policy convergence. Korea is using industrial policy to accelerate AI infrastructure outside congested regions. Canada is using national principles to align growth with public benefit. U.S. regulators are using transmission studies and state-level orders to manage reliability and speculative demand. Different tools, same direction: governments are no longer passive observers of AI infrastructure. They are becoming gatekeepers, co-investors, standard-setters and risk managers.

Industry Implications

For startups, the message is to sell into constraints, not narratives. A climate-tech company that helps data centers reduce water use, shift load, use waste heat, integrate storage or verify clean-power procurement has a sharper value proposition than a company that simply attaches itself to the AI theme. The buyer's urgent question is operational: can you help us get permitted, connected, financed or accepted by the community faster?

For enterprise buyers and hyperscalers, energy strategy is now corporate strategy. Power procurement cannot sit only inside facilities or sustainability teams. It affects geography, capital allocation, model deployment, customer commitments and public reputation. Enterprises planning AI adoption should understand where their compute will physically run, how exposed it is to electricity-price volatility, and whether vendors can demonstrate credible energy and water stewardship.

For governments, the task is sequencing. Too much friction pushes investment elsewhere. Too little scrutiny shifts costs to ratepayers and creates backlash. The best frameworks will separate bankable projects from speculative queue inflation, reward flexible load behaviour, link growth to new clean supply and ensure durable community benefits.

For investors, diligence needs to move upstream. AI infrastructure exposure should include questions that resemble project finance: power availability, interconnection status, transmission constraints, water permits, cooling architecture, grid-service obligations, offtake quality, community risk and equipment lead times. In many deals, the energy appendix may become more important than the pitch deck's market-size slide.

For Korean companies entering North America, the opportunity is practical and immediate. Canada and the United States need partners who can build, supply, finance and operate infrastructure under tighter constraints. Korean innovators can bring manufacturing discipline, electrical equipment, battery systems, digital-grid tools, cooling technologies and project execution. They will still need localized positioning, local service models, compliance readiness and references that translate Korean capability into North American trust.

Conclusion

What does this trend mean for North America? It means the region's AI advantage will depend on whether it can turn abundant ambition into deliverable infrastructure. The United States has massive demand, capital markets, hyperscalers and technology depth, but it also faces interconnection queues, transmission congestion and political pressure over electricity costs. Canada has clean power, nuclear expertise, hydro resources, AI talent and a relatively strong public-benefit framing, but it must avoid becoming only a low-carbon hosting site for foreign compute. The economic opportunity is larger than data centers: it includes grid modernization, advanced manufacturing, nuclear and SMR supply chains, power electronics, cooling, construction, software and community-benefit models.

The competitive advantage will go to jurisdictions that can say yes responsibly. That means fast but credible permitting, clear cost allocation, new clean generation, flexible-load rules, water stewardship and local industrial participation. For investors, the outlook is constructive but more selective. AI power demand is real, but not every announced project is equally real. Capital should favour companies and regions that can convert demand into energization.

Korean innovation can positively impact Canada in several concrete ways. Korean manufacturers and engineering firms can help address grid-equipment and battery-storage bottlenecks. Korean semiconductor, telecom and AI-infrastructure companies can partner with Canadian utilities, provinces and research institutions on responsible data-center deployment. Korean nuclear and SMR interest can complement Canada's own nuclear strategy through joint R&D, supply-chain collaboration and workforce development. Korean cooling, water-reuse and industrial-automation technologies can help Canadian projects meet higher expectations for environmental performance and community benefit.

The actionable insight is this: Canada should not view Korean AI and energy infrastructure capability only as foreign investment to attract. It should view Korea as a strategic industrial partner. For Korean companies, Canada can be a North American proving ground where clean power, policy discipline, AI talent and public R&D support create a credible first landing. For Canada, Korean partnerships can accelerate commercialization and turn responsible AI infrastructure from a policy aspiration into an exportable advantage.

The next AI infrastructure winners will not be the loudest announcers of capacity. They will be the operators, suppliers and regions that can deliver power, prove value and earn trust.
