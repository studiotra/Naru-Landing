The AI Power Stack: Why Korea and North America Are Rebuilding Energy Around Compute

By Naru · August 31, 2026 · Market Trends

Subtitle: AI is no longer just a software story. In Korea, Canada, and the United States, compute growth is forcing a new industrial equation around firm clean power, transmission, storage, and strategic site selection.

The global AI race is beginning to look less like a competition over models and more like a competition over megawatts. Chips still matter. Talent still matters. Capital still matters. But the binding constraint for the next phase of AI infrastructure is increasingly physical: electricity that can be delivered at industrial scale, around the clock, without turning public ratepayers or climate goals into collateral damage.

That shift is visible on both sides of the Pacific. South Korea is revising national power-demand forecasts after semiconductor clusters, AI data centers, and physical AI projects changed the country’s industrial load profile in a matter of months. Ontario is designing a data-centre approval regime around full cost recovery, digital sovereignty, and community benefits. The United States is using federal energy sites, dedicated generation, batteries, and new grid-market rules to accelerate AI campuses while containing reliability risk.

The common movement is not simply “AI uses a lot of power.” The stronger story is that power is becoming part of the product. Data-center developers, semiconductor manufacturers, cloud platforms, utilities, governments, and infrastructure investors are being pulled into one strategic stack: compute, clean firm energy, grid access, storage, land, water, permitting, and public legitimacy. For Korean innovators looking toward North America, and for Canadian leaders trying to turn clean electricity into economic advantage, this is a market-entry signal hiding in plain sight.

Signals from Korea

South Korea’s latest policy debate is unusually direct: the country cannot separate AI leadership from power-system planning. Reuters reported in July that Korea is preparing a record 2027 budget of more than 800 trillion won, supported in part by stronger tax revenues from the AI chip boom. The government named chips, AI data centers, and physical AI as three fiscal-priority “mega-projects,” and President Lee Jae Myung said public resources would be used to keep corporate investments on schedule.

That fiscal signal was followed by a sharper infrastructure signal. Korea JoongAng Daily reported that the country’s projected 2040 peak electricity demand has been revised upward by as much as 26.8 gigawatts compared with an estimate made only four months earlier. The revision reflects the Honam semiconductor cluster, expansions in Pyeongtaek and Cheongju, the Yongin semiconductor cluster, and AI data-center investment plans. In practical terms, the increase is roughly equivalent to the capacity of 19 large APR1400 reactors.

The number matters less as a nuclear talking point than as a planning shock. Korea’s current peak demand is around 100 gigawatts; the new 2040 projection reaches 158.4 to 165 gigawatts. Advanced semiconductor fabs and AI data centers do not behave like flexible consumer demand. They require high-quality, highly reliable electricity every hour of the day. A manufacturing cluster can be delayed by power availability just as surely as by land, water, permits, or financing.

At an August public policy forum for the 12th Basic Plan for Long-Term Electricity Supply and Demand, Korean officials and experts framed electricity as a national strategic asset. Aju Press reported that Climate, Energy and Environment Minister Kim Sung-hwan described Korea as facing the climate crisis and AI revolution at the same time. His proposed direction was not a return to coal or oil, but a clean-energy mix in which renewables form the center and nuclear supplies firm support for AI data centers, robots, and other electrified industries.

The renewable ambition is substantial. BusinessKorea reported that the government has discussed expanding commercial renewable capacity from 37 gigawatts in 2025 to 220 gigawatts by 2040, including major growth in solar and offshore wind. Yet the same analysis noted the commercial dilemma: even a sixfold increase in renewables could cover only about 40% of projected 2040 electricity consumption when intermittency is considered. The hard problem is not whether renewables matter. It is how to combine renewables, nuclear, gas transition capacity, storage, HVDC transmission, virtual power plants, and demand-location planning into a system that can support heavy industrial load.

There is also a commercialization signal beyond Korea’s borders. TerraPower announced agreements in August with Hyundai Engineering & Construction and SK Innovation to commercialize Natrium advanced reactor technology across the United States, Korea, and selected international markets. Hyundai E&C was selected as EPC contractor for up to eight future Natrium reactors, while SK Innovation signed a term sheet to explore Korea’s first commercial Natrium plant. The relevance to AI is explicit: advanced nuclear is being positioned as flexible, carbon-free capacity for power-intensive digital infrastructure.

For Korean companies, this is a strategic opening. Korea already has depth in semiconductors, batteries, shipbuilding, heavy engineering, power equipment, nuclear construction, and industrial automation. The AI power crunch turns those capabilities into exportable infrastructure services, not just domestic industrial assets. North American data centers may need Korean chips, but they may also need Korean EPC discipline, battery-system integration, grid hardware, advanced materials, construction execution, and operating know-how.

North American Perspective

North America is experiencing the same constraint through a different institutional structure. In Canada, the most visible recent example is Ontario’s Data Centre Playbook, released on August 13. The province wants data centers because they can support AI adoption, domestic data storage, tech employment, and broader economic growth. But Ontario is also making clear that grid access will not be treated as an entitlement.

The playbook proposes that data centers pay the full cost of their impact on the electricity system. New facilities above 1 megawatt would face a separate, higher rate class than large industrial users, and the province says it will prioritize projects that bring their own generation, deliver community benefits, protect digital sovereignty, and meet environmental standards around water and noise. Ontario is offering non-financial support - a clean grid, skilled workforce, land, and permitting facilitation - rather than direct subsidies.

That is a subtle but important policy design choice. Ontario is not rejecting data centers; it is raising the standard for what counts as value-adding digital infrastructure. The province is effectively asking proponents to prove that a data center strengthens the economy, protects Canadian data, pays its own way, and earns public confidence. For hyperscalers and infrastructure funds, this means power economics and community legitimacy belong in the investment model from day one.

The United States is leaning more heavily on federal land, dedicated generation, and market redesign. In July, the U.S. Department of Energy announced a partnership with Brookfield, NextEra Energy, Big Rivers Electric Power, Jackson Purchase Energy Cooperative, and Paducah Power System to redevelop part of DOE’s Paducah site in Kentucky into a major AI and high-performance computing campus. The proposed investment exceeds $100 billion. The energy plan includes 2 gigawatts of new gas-fired generation, transmission upgrades, and up to 2.6 gigawatts of battery storage to support a 1.8-gigawatt AI campus.

Paducah is not a clean-energy purity story; it is an infrastructure realism story. The project pairs AI load with dedicated generation and storage at a former federal energy site with land, water, transmission, and fiber advantages. It also reflects a ratepayer-protection logic: new generation and storage should exceed campus demand and provide excess electricity to the regional grid. Whether that model satisfies climate objectives is debatable, but the commercial logic is clear. AI campuses are being designed as energy projects as much as real-estate or cloud projects.

Grid operators are also changing the rules. POWER Magazine reported that PJM Interconnection, the largest U.S. grid operator, filed an August proposal with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission to create an Interim Resource Adequacy Service, a Large Load Registry, and a “Bring Your Own New Capacity” mechanism. New large loads of at least 50 megawatts that enter service after June 1, 2027 could connect without fully secured capacity, but unsupported demand could be curtailed before broader emergency demand-response measures during grid stress.

This is a market design reset. PJM is trying to avoid a situation in which data centers consume scarce capacity while other customers absorb reliability and cost risks. The proposed registry would improve visibility into large loads. The capacity mechanism would reward projects that bring real incremental supply. The curtailment structure would make flexibility part of the bargain. For data-center developers, the message is direct: speed to power now depends on your capacity strategy.

Advanced nuclear is re-entering the conversation because AI load needs firm, low-carbon electricity. Bloomberg reported that TerraPower expects to announce a second U.S. nuclear project intended for a data center, with construction targeted for 2027. TechCrunch added useful technical context: TerraPower’s Natrium design pairs a 345-megawatt sodium-cooled reactor with molten-salt energy storage, allowing the plant to keep the reactor running while shifting output to match demand. That kind of flexibility is attractive in a grid increasingly shaped by both intermittent renewables and volatile data-center load.

Connecting the Dots

These developments are occurring simultaneously because AI has compressed the timelines of electricity planning. A data center or semiconductor fab can be proposed, financed, and partially built faster than a transmission line, reactor, offshore wind farm, or major substation can be permitted and constructed. Korea’s minister captured the asymmetry plainly: chips and AI data centers can be set up in two to three years if a company is determined; preparing electricity takes much longer.

The result is a new industrial sequencing problem. The old model assumed that electricity was a utility input procured after a site was chosen. The emerging model treats power as a co-equal site-selection criterion. Land without power is stranded. Clean power without transmission is stranded. A generation project without an offtaker may be unfinanceable. A data center without credible cost allocation can lose regulatory and community support. The winners will be the organizations that integrate all of these pieces early.

AI is the demand catalyst, but manufacturing is the multiplier. Korea’s demand shock comes from both AI data centers and semiconductor clusters. North America’s demand shock includes hyperscale compute, battery factories, reshored manufacturing, electrified industrial processes, and defense-adjacent technology supply chains. In both regions, power demand is no longer a smooth GDP-linked curve. It is arriving in concentrated blocks: 50 megawatts, 300 megawatts, 1 gigawatt, 10 gigawatts.

That changes investment behavior. Utilities and grid operators need better large-load forecasting. Governments need permitting regimes that distinguish strategic projects from speculative queue-squatting. Developers need PPAs, behind-the-meter generation, storage, demand flexibility, and credible plans for water and heat. Investors need to underwrite transformers, turbines, batteries, grid software, interconnection deposits, and construction execution with the same seriousness they apply to model performance or cloud utilization.

It also widens the opportunity set for Korean-North American collaboration. Korea’s strength is not only in building products; it is in industrializing complex technology. Canada’s strength is not only in clean electricity; it is in combining hydro, nuclear, critical minerals, AI research, public R&D programs, and proximity to the U.S. market. The United States brings scale, federal energy sites, hyperscaler demand, and deep infrastructure capital. The practical opportunity sits between them: partner to build the physical layer of the AI economy.

Industry Implications

For startups, energy literacy is becoming go-to-market literacy. AI, climate tech, grid software, battery, nuclear-services, and industrial automation founders should be able to explain how their product affects load growth, interconnection, flexibility, carbon intensity, or operating cost. A pitch that ignores energy constraints will feel incomplete to utilities, data-center operators, and industrial customers. The strongest startup opportunities may emerge in the unglamorous middle: grid orchestration, thermal management, power electronics, long-duration storage, permitting analytics, load forecasting, power procurement software, and automation for construction and operations.

For enterprise buyers, procurement needs to integrate energy and supply-chain strategy. A cloud or AI-infrastructure decision is no longer only a question of compute price. It carries exposure to power markets, carbon accounting, data sovereignty, community opposition, and supplier-country risk. Enterprises should ask vendors where power comes from, who pays for grid upgrades, what happens during curtailment, and how expansion plans align with emissions commitments. Korean suppliers entering North America should prepare for these questions with localized proof, not generic sustainability language.

For governments, the core task is credible prioritization. Ontario’s playbook, PJM’s capacity framework, and Korea’s 12th power-plan debate all point toward the same governance challenge: not every large load can be treated equally. Public agencies need criteria for economic value, cost causation, emissions, community benefit, and strategic fit. They also need faster permitting for projects that meet those criteria, because slow approval processes can make clean infrastructure lose to faster fossil alternatives.

For investors, the investable thesis is moving downstream into infrastructure execution. AI infrastructure is capital intensive, but not all value will accrue to model companies or data-center owners. EPC firms, nuclear supply-chain companies, transformer manufacturers, battery integrators, power-market software vendors, water-treatment firms, and grid-connection specialists will become part of the AI value chain. The underwriting question is not only whether demand exists. It is whether a company can navigate interconnection, permitting, financing, supply chains, construction, and community acceptance.

For Korean companies considering North America, the market-entry bar is rising. Technical excellence is not enough. A credible North American motion now requires local partners, regulatory fluency, bankable references, financing structures, and a narrative that translates Korean industrial capability into North American outcomes. The companies that land well will not present themselves as distant vendors. They will show up as execution partners for power-constrained growth.

Conclusion

What does this trend mean for North America? It means the next phase of AI competitiveness will be decided partly by energy systems. Regions with clean, reliable, expandable power will have an advantage in attracting data centers, semiconductor activity, AI startups, and advanced manufacturing. But that advantage will only hold if governments and utilities protect ratepayers, accelerate strategic infrastructure, and give investors clear rules for large-load connections.

For Canada, the opportunity is especially concrete. Ontario, Quebec, Alberta, British Columbia, and other provinces each have different electricity profiles, but Canada has a credible claim to clean power, skilled talent, AI research, critical minerals, and proximity to U.S. customers. The challenge is fragmentation: provincial grids, uneven intertie capacity, permitting complexity, and political pressure around data-center impacts. If Canada can turn clean electricity into an integrated industrial offer - sovereign compute, low-carbon power, critical-mineral supply chains, battery storage, and applied AI talent - it can compete for higher-quality infrastructure investment rather than simply hosting commodity load.

Korean innovation could positively impact Canada in that transition. Hyundai E&C’s advanced nuclear construction role, SK Innovation’s energy and battery capabilities, Korea’s semiconductor ecosystem, and the country’s experience in large-scale industrial execution all map onto Canadian bottlenecks. Canada needs partners that can help commercialize clean firm power, deploy storage, scale manufacturing, strengthen critical-mineral and battery supply chains, and move from pilot projects to bankable infrastructure. Korea needs North American beachheads where its technology and execution capability can earn local trust.

The actionable insight is straightforward: treat AI infrastructure as a cross-border industrial platform, not a single-sector boom. Canadian governments should design partnership channels for Korean energy, battery, nuclear, grid, and semiconductor firms. Korean companies should enter Canada with localized commercialization plans, not export-only assumptions. Investors should look for companies that connect compute demand to power delivery. The market is no longer asking who can build the largest model. It is asking who can power the economy that model requires.

The AI infrastructure race is becoming a power-infrastructure race. The advantage will go to regions and companies that can combine clean electrons, industrial execution, and public trust.
