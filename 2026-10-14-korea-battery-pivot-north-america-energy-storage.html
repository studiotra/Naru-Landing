Idle EV Lines, Hungry Grids: Korea's Battery Pivot Is Becoming North America's Storage Supply Chain

By Naru · October 14, 2026

Korea's battery makers built North American factories for an electric-vehicle boom that stalled. This autumn those plants are being retooled, line by line, to supply the grid, just as Washington and Ottawa decide whose batteries they are willing to buy.

The dates tell the story. On Sept. 21, LG Energy Solution declared its Lansing, Michigan plant in full mass production, completing a network of five energy-storage sites across the United States and Canada. On Sept. 22, the American Clean Power Association reported a record quarter for U.S. battery installations and noted that "manufacturers are repurposing idle EV cell capacity." On Sept. 30, LG signed a Canadian integrator, Eneon, to supply North American-made systems. This month Samsung SDI's first U.S. lithium-iron-phosphate line starts producing in Indiana, and on Oct. 9 Ontario published a draft procurement that would reward Canadian content in grid batteries. None of this is an EV story any longer. It is the story of how one country's industrial overcapacity is becoming another continent's energy infrastructure.

Signals from Korea

The first signal is physical. LG Energy Solution's Lansing plant, originally planned as a third joint venture with General Motors, was bought out and redesigned as a mixed site: more than 35 GWh of annual capacity making pouch LFP cells for storage today, North America's first prismatic LFP cells in 2027 and EV cells for Toyota alongside. With Lansing running, LG operates five storage sites in the region, two in Michigan, NextStar Energy in Windsor, Ontario, and joint-venture plants with Honda in Ohio and GM in Tennessee, and targets more than 50 GWh of North American LFP capacity by year-end, about 80 percent of its global storage capacity. The Honda plant was built for EVs; its first cells, produced in July, went into stationary storage.

The second signal is that the localized value chain has become the product. LG booked more than 3 trillion won of storage orders in the first half, including AI data-centre projects, and on Sept. 22 its JF2 AC Link system qualified as "DSX Ready" in Nvidia's AI-factory reference programme, the only Korean battery maker among the initial suppliers. Eight days later it signed Eneon, a Canadian system integrator, to supply the North American-made JF2 DC Link, a containerized LFP unit storing up to 5.1 MWh. Eneon's explanation was telling: the deal lets it offer a "Tier 1 BESS product manufactured in the U.S." to "projects seeking alignment with ITC and domestic content requirements." LG's 5.94 trillion won contract in March to supply Lansing-made prismatic cells for Tesla's Megapack 3 from August 2027 set the template.

The third signal is Samsung SDI, which is converting three of four lines at its StarPlus Energy joint venture with Stellantis in Kokomo, Indiana from EV cells to prismatic LFP for storage, lifting plant capacity from about 33 GWh to 40 GWh because an LFP line yields 10 to 12 GWh where an NCA line yielded 7 to 8. The first LFP line starts in October for the Samsung Battery Box 2.0; the second is reserved for Tesla from the first half of 2027; the third serves a global integrator. The Elec reported that about 80 percent of the plant's volume through 2029 is sold and that the mothballed Synergy Cells site in Indiana, wholly owned after GM's exit, will be revived as an LFP storage plant for late 2028. Samsung SDI returned to profit in the second quarter after seven losing quarters, citing data-centre battery demand.

The fourth signal is SK On, which had no overseas storage order this year until Aug. 31, when it signed a 9 GWh, five-year LFP contract worth about 1.5 trillion won with NeoVolta Power, to be produced on converted lines in Georgia, with a second 9 GWh tranche in negotiation. SK On has signed LFP cathode agreements with Posco Future M and L&F, so the Korean materials chain is following the cell makers across the Pacific. At home the same logic applies: SK On won half of Korea's second government storage tender in February by committing to make LFP cells in Seosan rather than import them.

The fifth signal is sovereign capital. On Sept. 30, the U.S. Commerce Department and the Korean government selected Project Star, a USD22.3 billion, 6,472 MW gas-fired campus in Encinal, Texas, built to power an adjacent 5 GW data-centre campus, as the first project under Korea's USD200 billion strategic investment commitment. No Korean battery maker has been named a supplier, but pairing gas generation with batteries near data centres is "becoming almost commonplace," iM Securities analyst Jung Won-seok told the Korea Herald. Korean state money is now co-investing in the load centres Korean battery plants intend to serve.

Korea's battery makers did not choose the storage market; the EV slowdown chose it for them. What they chose was to convert plants rather than close them, and that decision is now worth more than any new factory announcement.

North American Perspective

In the United States, demand is no longer the question. ACP and Wood Mackenzie recorded 5.4 GW and 18.9 GWh of storage installed in the second quarter, a record in energy terms and up 17 percent year on year even as power capacity fell, because average duration rose from 2.8 to 3.5 hours. SEIA and Benchmark counted just under 31 GWh in the first half, with commercial demand "shaped by the rapid expansion of data centers." Wood Mackenzie forecasts 207 GW and 715 GWh cumulative by 2031, and its analyst Allison Feeney argued that storage will "enable speed to power for data centers" facing grid-connection bottlenecks.

Supply is where policy has intervened. On Jan. 1 the Section 301 tariff on Chinese non-EV lithium-ion batteries rose from 7.5 to 25 percent. Prohibited-foreign-entity rules now apply to storage projects that began construction after Dec. 31, 2025, with thresholds stepping up in 2027, yet Treasury's interim guidance left core definitions unresolved and safe-harbour tables are not due until Dec. 31. On Aug. 26 Executive Order 14421 declared a national emergency over the bulk-power system and authorized the Energy Department to prohibit battery storage, inverters and UPS equipment tied to covered foreign entities; the department's request for information closed Oct. 9, with rules due within 120 days. Wood Mackenzie expects utility-scale deployment to "see some stagnation in 2026 and 2027 as the industry adapts," then grow 11 percent a year from 2028 "as domestic cell manufacturing ramps up."

That domestic ramp is largely Korean. SEIA puts planned North American storage-cell manufacturing above 250 GWh, with LG Energy Solution the largest at 44 GWh of nameplate capacity by end-2026 and more than 100 GWh by 2030. The exception proves the pattern: Tesla shipped its first Megapacks with Nevada-made LFP cells in July and began producing Megapack 3 at a 50 GWh Megafactory in Brookshire, Texas in August, yet it has contracted LG for Lansing cells from 2027 and anchors Samsung SDI's Indiana lines. The American integrator champion is building its domestic supply chain on Korean cells.

Canada's demand signal is Ontario. In June the IESO awarded all three capacity contracts in its LT2 window to battery projects, 640 MW at CAD563.48 per MW-business day, 36 percent below the previous expedited procurement, taking the province past 3,500 MW of storage by 2030. On Oct. 9 it published draft documents for the second window which, in bracketed language, offer an evaluated price reduction of 1, 1.5 or 2 percent for proponents committing to 60, 70 or 80 percent Canadian content in construction materials and labour. The provisions remain "under ongoing consideration and policy development," with final documents due in the first quarter of 2027. Alberta added a data point on Oct. 8 when Northland Power's 80 MW Jurassic project, the province's largest, came online under a 15-year offtake.

Canada's supply signal is a single plant, and it is Korean-owned. NextStar Energy in Windsor, built as an LG-Stellantis joint venture for EV cells, began producing LFP storage cells in November 2025, became wholly owned by LG when Stellantis sold its 49 percent stake in February, and started a pack line on June 25, giving Canada cell-to-pack storage manufacturing with up to 49.5 GWh of capacity. Both countries now treat batteries as the fastest capacity they can buy. The difference is the machinery around supply: Washington has tariffs, tax rules and an emergency order; Ottawa has a zero tariff on cells, a content incentive still in brackets and one factory whose storage strategy is set in Seoul.

Connecting the Dots

Why is this happening now, on both sides of the Pacific at once? The first force is that stranded capacity met stranded demand. Korean makers sized their North American plants to 2022 EV forecasts and were left with idle lines and seven quarters of losses. Converting a line to LFP costs a fraction of a greenfield plant and, as Samsung SDI's Kokomo numbers show, increases output per line. At the same moment, data centres needed power faster than grids could connect it, and batteries became the quickest deliverable capacity. The two problems solved each other.

The second force is that trade policy built a protected pool of demand. Chinese producers dominate global LFP, the chemistry storage requires. Section 301 tariffs, the prohibited-foreign-entity thresholds and Executive Order 14421 together make Chinese cells progressively harder to finance, import or install in the United States. Korean companies are the only scaled non-Chinese LFP producers with plants inside the tariff wall, so compliance itself has become their product, which is how Eneon described the LG deal. The cost is margin: LFP is a commodity chemistry, and Korean makers accepted it because utilization now matters more than price per cell.

The third force is that the battery has moved from the vehicle to the AI factory. Nvidia now certifies storage systems the way it certifies networking; Korean state capital is co-financing a Texas gas-and-data-centre campus; Samsung SDI credits data-centre demand for its return to profit. When the largest buyers of electricity are hyperscalers rather than utilities, batteries become part of a compute reference design, and a supply chain built for cars reorganizes around compute.

Industry Implications

For startups, the opening is in integration, compliance and controls. Eneon shows that a small Canadian integrator can secure Tier 1 supply by solving a buyer's eligibility problem rather than its chemistry problem. Software for data-centre load smoothing, domestically made grid-forming inverters, fire-safety systems that score in Korean and Ontario tenders, and documentation tools for material-assistance ratios all sit in the gap between a Korean cell and a bankable project.

For enterprise, cell supply is becoming a contracted asset. Samsung SDI has sold roughly 80 percent of its Indiana output through 2029; Tesla has locked LG's Lansing lines for three years. Developers and utilities without compliant cells for 2027 and 2028 are now negotiating against hyperscalers for the remainder. The practical response is Tesla's two-track model: own or contract domestic cells, and keep a second qualified supplier.

For governments, the job is to finish the rules. In the United States, Treasury's missing definitions and the Energy Department's pending order are the reason Wood Mackenzie forecasts stagnation before growth; delay converts a supply policy into a deployment pause. In Canada, Ontario's bracketed content incentive is the only lever that would connect Windsor's cells to Ontario's procurement, and it is undecided.

For investors, the diligence questions have changed. Utilization rates on converted lines, the capital cost of conversion versus new build, the share of output contracted to a single customer, and exposure to the 2027 threshold step-up matter more than nameplate capacity. The second-order winners are visible: cathode suppliers such as Posco Future M and L&F, and the equipment makers for whom Synergy Cells alone could mean around 1.5 trillion won of orders.

Conclusion

What does this trend mean for North America? It means the region is acquiring, at Korean expense and on an accelerated timetable, the piece of the clean-energy supply chain it most conspicuously lacked: scaled LFP cell production outside China. The economic opportunity is a storage manufacturing base planned above 250 GWh, anchored by plants that exist rather than announcements. The competitive advantage is timing: compliant domestic cells arrive in 2027 and 2028, exactly when trade rules make them mandatory and data-centre load makes storage the fastest capacity on offer. The industrial implication is that last cycle's EV plants will be grid assets, not stranded ones. The investment outlook is constructive for cell makers with contracted offtake, integrators who sell compliance and the materials chain behind them, and selective on developers whose 2027 pipelines still depend on uncertain guidance.

Canada's position is favourable but passive. It hosts a cell plant, procures storage at falling prices and is drafting a content incentive. Yet the plant is Korean-owned and the incentive is in brackets. Whether Canada becomes a node in this supply chain or merely a market for it will be decided over the next 18 months.

How could Korean innovation positively impact Canada? Four pathways stand out.

First, manufacturing. Windsor is now a cell-to-pack storage plant. If Ontario confirms its Canadian-content incentive before the LT2 second window closes in 2027, Windsor-made systems become the natural bid for the remaining capacity contracts. Ontario and Ottawa should treat NextStar as strategic storage infrastructure rather than an EV legacy, and work with LG on a prismatic LFP line in Windsor to mirror Lansing.

Second, commercialization. The Eneon model is replicable: Canadian integrators that pair Korean North American-made cells with their own controls and delivery capability can sell into the United States under CUSMA as compliant Tier 1 product, and into Canadian procurements that increasingly ask about origin. They should be in Seoul this winter qualifying supply for 2027 and 2028.

Third, critical minerals. LFP cathodes need lithium, iron and phosphate, and cells need graphite; Korean cathode makers following SK On to North America need non-Chinese sources of all four. A Korea-Canada materials framework aimed at the storage chain, rather than at faded EV ambitions, would give Korean suppliers feedstock and Canadian miners a buyer with plants two provinces away.

Fourth, policy and R&D. Korea's storage tenders score fire safety and industrial contribution alongside price; Ontario is designing content rules; both governments want non-Chinese LFP supply. A joint program on safety standards, grid-forming performance and local-content accounting would give Canadian regulators a tested template and Korean makers a second North American jurisdiction that recognizes their investment.

The actionable insight for Canadian leaders is that Korean storage capacity in North America is being allocated now, in supply agreements running to 2029 and 2030. Buyers, integrators and governments that sign within the next two procurement cycles will have domestic supply locked in before the compliance wall rises; those that wait will buy what is left.

The battery supply chain North America spent a decade trying to build for cars is arriving instead for the grid, and it is arriving with Korean nameplates. The countries that recognize what they have been handed will treat it as infrastructure to secure, not imports to count.
