From Queue to Auction: Korea and North America Rewrite the Rules of Grid Access

By Naru · November 4, 2026

Korea has reopened its most congested region to new renewable projects after a two-year freeze and will stop handing out grid connections on a first-come basis from January 2027. Six US regional grid operators must file new rules for connecting data centres by Nov. 16, after federal regulators threw out the largest one's plan. Québec has allocated 11 megawatts to data centres in almost four years against 2,500 megawatts of requests. The scarce asset in clean energy is no longer the megawatt. It is the right to connect it.

For most of the past decade the energy transition was discussed as a supply problem. Over the past five weeks the policy record on both sides of the Pacific has shifted to a different question: who gets onto the wire, in what order, on what terms, and who pays. Korea's answer is a Cabinet-approved overhaul that replaces queues with auctions and firm access with curtailable access. North America's is arriving piecemeal through FERC dockets, provincial regulators and system operators, but it is converging on the same toolkit. Grid access has become a conditional, allocated asset, and the jurisdictions that allocate it well will capture the next cycle of industrial investment.

Signals from Korea

The structural signal came on Sept. 29, when the Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment briefed the State Council on a Power Grid Innovation Plan built to absorb more than 100 gigawatts of renewables by 2030. From Oct. 1 it lifted the "grid-management substation" designations that had blocked new connections across Honam, the south-western region where developers had been told nothing could connect until after 2034, and it raises transmission-level hosting capacity from 119 to 171 gigawatts nationally and from 37 to 64 gigawatts in Honam. The more consequential change is procedural. Connection rights, granted free to whoever filed first, will from January 2027 be awarded only to projects that win the restructured renewable-energy auction, scored on economics, public benefit and grid conditions. The ministry has already clawed back about 9 gigawatts of rights from phantom projects, expects to recover 10 gigawatts more in Honam by deferring delayed offshore wind, and from 2031 will allocate capacity assuming a 67.2 percent realization rate.

The second signal is that Korea has decided curtailability is a currency. Non-firm access, under which a solar project connects above a line's rated limit and accepts curtailment during congestion, raises hosting ceilings by 15 to 20 percent, enough for 2.6 gigawatts of additional Honam solar without new wires. A "self-managed access" scheme will let developers install two to three times the rated grid capacity if storage keeps exports within the permitted band. Storage is also being bought as a substitute for wires: 3 gigawatts of extra solar through distribution-network batteries and 1.6 gigawatts through substation batteries by 2030. The procurement is live: the 2026 ESS central contract market is buying 1,100 megawatts and 6,600 megawatt-hours of mainland storage on 15-year fixed-price contracts, with bidding closing Nov. 11 and awards due this month, and half the score goes to non-price factors including grid-stabilization contribution, fire safety and domestic industrial content.

The third signal is the backbone. The West Coast Energy Highway, four 2-gigawatt HVDC submarine lines carrying Honam power 1,070 kilometres to the Seoul region by 2038, has moved from policy to procurement. On Oct. 7 KEPCO pre-published bidding rules for the first 220-kilometre, 525-kilovolt Saemangeum–Seohwaseong section, worth about 1.2 trillion won, with the tender due in the first quarter of 2027 and completion pulled forward a year to December 2030. Bidders may qualify on similar rather than identical 525-kilovolt experience in exchange for a ten-year warranty, but must hold full test certificates, production facilities and cable-laying vessels by mid-2028. LS Cable & System and Taihan Cable & Solution are the cable contenders, while Hyosung Heavy Industries, HD Hyundai Electric, LS Electric and Iljin Electric are localizing converters and transformers through a special-purpose company with KEPCO whose stated purpose is an export track record; the K-GX strategy names direct-current transmission as an industry Korea intends to export.

Korea's grid reform adds no new power plant. It changes who may connect, in what order and on what terms, and treats a battery and a curtailment agreement as substitutes for a transmission line. That is market design, and it is exportable.

North American Perspective

The United States is working the same problem from the opposite end of the wire, where the queue is load waiting for generation and a path. In June FERC found the large-load interconnection rules of six regional grid operators appeared inadequate, including on shielding existing customers from upgrade costs, and gave them until Nov. 16 to respond. On Oct. 9 it rejected most of PJM's answer: the Interim Resource Adequacy Service, which would have let data centres connect without matching new capacity if they agreed to be curtailed before grid emergencies, intruded on state authority over retail service and appeared discriminatory, the Commission found. Commissioner David Rosner's concurrence set out the division of labour: states decide who is curtailed first, and PJM operates to those rules.

The same day FERC approved, 4-1, Duke Energy Carolinas' tariff requiring loads of 100 megawatts or more to pay a committed revenue contribution equal to 75 percent of scheduled demand for 15 years after their upgrades enter service. In MISO, a 120-day fast-track study for loads above 200 megawatts paired with nearby generation drew opposition on Oct. 23 from 11 of 15 state commissions and Google over cost shifting and utility gatekeeping. On Oct. 28 the California ISO board approved a "flexible interim load interconnection" that lets a large load energize quickly with enforceable flexibility until upgrades are complete, backed by hourly telemetry and remote disconnection. California has, in effect, adopted Korea's non-firm access for data centres.

The scale of the queue was illustrated on Oct. 13, when PJM filed a study agreement for SoftBank's Flagship project in Ohio: roughly 10 gigawatts of data-centre load, 10,170 megawatts of new gas generation through 2032 and three 765-kilovolt lines. The filing grants no interconnection rights, and SB Energy has committed to pay for the infrastructure regardless of how much power the campus uses.

Canada's queue is provincial and even more tightly rationed. In Québec, where connections above 5 megawatts have needed ministerial approval since February 2023, Hydro-Québec disclosed at an Oct. 5 Régie de l'énergie hearing that just 11 of the 1,500 megawatts allocated since then went to data centres, while requests under review reached 5,390 megawatts at Sept. 30, about 2,500 from data centres. The utility wants to raise the data-centre rate from 6.8 to 13 cents a kilowatt-hour, the province scores applicants on seven criteria, and no new generation arrives before 2028. Alberta allocated its entire 1,200-megawatt interim large-load limit to two data centres and in June codified "bring your own generation" in a regulation that tethers a data centre's connection to new generation; the Phase 2A intake for further capacity has slipped from August to "this fall." Ontario's IESO answered stakeholders on Nov. 2 and presents market-rule amendments for large computational loads on Nov. 19.

Ottawa's contribution is wires between the queues. On Oct. 13 Energy Minister Tim Hodgson announced nearly C$270 million for New Brunswick, including C$50 million for a STATCOM at the Salisbury terminal, following the National Electricity Strategy's goal of doubling grid capacity by 2050 and June's designation of five priority interties: an 800-kilometre, ±200-kilovolt HVDC line from British Columbia to Yukon, upgraded Alberta–BC and Alberta–Saskatchewan links, a Saskatchewan–Manitoba expansion of up to 2 gigawatts, and new submarine cables between Prince Edward Island and New Brunswick from 2028.

Connecting the Dots

Why is this happening in the same quarter on both sides of the Pacific? Because the physical constraint is identical even though the queues point in opposite directions. Korea's ministry cited a global connection backlog of roughly 2,500 gigawatts in 2025, including 1,700 gigawatts of renewables, 600 of batteries and 150 of data centres, and that a substation takes six years to build and a 345-kilovolt line more than nine, against one or two for a solar farm or a data centre. When the thing being connected is built five times faster than the wire, first-come-first-served allocation produces hoarded rights, phantom projects and a paper queue in front of real demand.

The responses converge on five instruments. Curtailability as a currency: Korea's non-firm access, California's flexible interim interconnection and PJM's rejected IRAS all trade a right to disconnect for a faster connection, and FERC objected to PJM's jurisdiction, not the concept. Allocation by merit rather than arrival: Korea's 2027 auction, Québec's seven criteria and Alberta's capped allocation replace the queue with a scoring sheet. The newcomer pays and proves: KEPCO's cost-sharing, Duke's 15-year commitment, Hydro-Québec's 13-cent rate and SB Energy's take-or-pay pledge are one principle in four dialects. Storage as a wire substitute: Korea is buying 4.6 gigawatts of hosting capacity through batteries, while Alberta's tethering lets storage count as offsetting generation. And a direct-current backbone with industrial policy attached: the West Coast Energy Highway, Canada's interties and AEP's 765-kilovolt lines all bet that long-distance transmission, not local generation, relieves the queue.

The differences matter too. Korea has one planner and one network owner, so it can rewrite national connection rules at a single Cabinet meeting; North America's authority is split among federal regulators, states, provinces and system operators, as the PJM order showed. Korea's advantage is speed of rule-making and an HVDC supply chain already shipping to the United States; North America's are capital, land and demand. The market is already pairing them: LS Cable's US$681 million HVDC submarine-cable plant in Chesapeake, Virginia, due to start production in early 2028, and Taihan's 320-kilovolt HVDC award in the Silicon Valley load pocket are Korean capacity built for the North American queue.

Industry Implications

For startups, the product is now the connection itself. Every instrument above needs software and hardware that had no market when access was firm and free: curtailment forecasting and dispatch for non-firm solar, telemetry and remote-disconnect compliance for flexible loads, battery dispatch against hosting limits and queue analytics. Korea's self-managed access and California's flexible interim interconnection create regulatory demand for exactly this stack, and Korea's 50-percent non-price scoring rewards vendors that can document grid value and fire safety rather than price alone.

For enterprise buyers, grid access is now a balance-sheet item. A data centre in Ohio, the Carolinas or Alberta should expect to bring or finance generation, accept conditional energization and underwrite upgrades with take-or-pay obligations; one in Québec should expect to compete on seven criteria and pay roughly double the legacy rate. Korean renewable developers face the mirror image: from 2027 a project without a winning auction bid has no connection, so bankability shifts from site control to auction readiness.

For governments, Korea's lesson is to make the allocation rule explicit before the queue forms. Seoul had to recover 9 gigawatts of phantom rights and freeze a region for two years before moving to auctions, realistic realization rates and cost-sharing. Provinces and states that publish criteria, cap and recycle allocations, and let flexible connections run ahead of upgrades will attract projects that can live with those terms.

For investors, the scarce asset has moved from the resource to the right. Projects holding firm, dated connection rights command a premium over equivalent resources without them, and queue positions carry claw-back risk that did not exist two years ago. The equipment chain that relieves the constraint, HVDC cable and converters, large transformers, grid-scale storage and STATCOMs, has order visibility through 2030 on both continents.

Conclusion

What does this trend mean for North America? The economic opportunity is that grid access, once an administrative formality, is now the gating factor for the largest capital programme on the continent, and the jurisdictions that allocate it transparently and quickly will win the siting decisions. The competitive advantage lies with regions that have both surplus clean energy and a credible path to connect it: Québec and Manitoba hydro, Alberta's gas-and-storage pairing, the US wind belt, and the Maritimes once the interties are built. The industrial implication is a durable domestic market for transmission equipment and storage that persists however AI forecasts move, because the backlog of generation waiting for wires is larger than the backlog of load. The investment outlook favours owners of firm connection rights, contracted storage and the HVDC supply chain, and is cautious on projects whose value rests on a queue position regulators are now recycling.

How could Korean innovation positively impact Canada? Four pathways are concrete.

First, equipment for the interties. Canada's priority list includes an 800-kilometre HVDC line, a 2-gigawatt prairie expansion and new submarine cables, and the country does not manufacture 525-kilovolt HVDC cable or large converter stations. Korea's cable makers are qualifying to that specification under KEPCO's 2027 tender, LS Cable's Virginia plant will be in production in 2028 inside the North American trade perimeter, and Hyosung, HD Hyundai Electric and LS Electric are localizing converters and transformers. A procurement framework that invites Korean suppliers to bid in consortia with Canadian EPC firms, or to site a second North American plant in a port province, would shorten delivery on every priority intertie.

Second, connection-rule transfer. Korea's non-firm access, self-managed access and storage-as-wire programme are operating regulations with published capacity figures, not pilots. Ontario's IESO, Alberta's AESO and Hydro-Québec are all writing large-load rules this quarter; a regulatory exchange built on KEPCO's operating data would let Canadian operators adopt flexible connections for data centres on evidence rather than theory.

Third, storage procurement design. Korea's central contract market, with 15-year fixed prices and half the score on grid contribution, safety and industrial content, is a template provinces could adapt for the battery capacity their intertie plans assume, and Korean battery makers with North American cell plants would qualify under a domestic-content screen.

Fourth, joint demonstration. The West Coast SPC exists to generate an export track record for Korean HVDC technology, and the BC–Yukon line is a natural reference project, financed jointly by the Canada Infrastructure Bank and Korea's policy lenders, which have already committed 300 billion won to LS Cable's Virginia plant.

The actionable insight for Canadian leaders is that the next round of energy investment will be decided by connection rules, not resource maps. Provinces that publish allocation criteria, let flexible connections precede upgrades and procure HVDC and storage on firm timelines will attract both the loads and the Korean equipment and capital already moving toward North America.

Korea reopened Honam, replaced the queue with an auction and put its cable makers on a 2028 deadline. FERC gave US grid operators until Nov. 16 to answer the same question. The wire is being rationed on both sides of the Pacific, and the jurisdictions that write the rules first, and build the hardware to relax them, will own the next decade of deployment.
